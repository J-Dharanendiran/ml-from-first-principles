# 🧭 PCA Geometric Intuition: Turning the Axes to Find Where the Data Actually Lives

> *"More columns do not mean more information. PCA's promise is to find the few directions that matter, without breaking the geometry of your data."*

---

## 🕳️ Why PCA Had to Exist

Rewind to the problem that started this whole story. You have a dataset with many input features, and you assume more features will mean a better model.

It does not, and the reasons form a chain:

```text
Larger feature space  →  Sparse data  →  Less informative distances  →  Harder learning
```

This is the **Curse of Dimensionality**. Along the way it also brings:

- 📈 **increased model complexity**
- 💻 **increased computational cost**
- 📉 **potentially reduced model performance**

> 💭 *"So how do I get out of this?"*

With **Dimensionality Reduction**: reduce the number of input dimensions while **retaining useful information** from the original data. One of the most widely used ways to do it is **PCA**.

---

## 🔍 So What Exactly Is PCA?

**PCA** stands for **Principal Component Analysis**. In short:

1. It is an **unsupervised machine learning technique** used for dimensionality reduction. *Unsupervised* means we have input data but **no output labels**.
2. It is also a **feature extraction technique**: it **transforms the original features into new features** called **principal components**.
3. Its main objective is to **reduce the number of dimensions** in a dataset while **preserving as much of the important information as possible**.

For example, a dataset with **100 features** might be represented, after PCA, by only **10 new features**. The dataset gets smaller, but it keeps its important characteristics.

> 💭 *"Is PCA just deleting the columns I do not need?"*

**No.** This is the single most important thing to hold on to:

> **PCA is not simply deleting unnecessary columns. It creates new mathematical features from combinations of the original features.**

One more honest note from the instructor: PCA is established and widely used, but its underlying mathematics is **quite complex**. So the focus here is on **intuition and how to use it**, not on deriving every calculation.

## 📸 The Photographer at the Football Match

To build that intuition, the instructor uses an analogy.

Imagine a **football match inside a 3D stadium**, and a **photographer** who wants to capture the whole match in a **2D photograph**.

The problem: one photograph cannot capture every detail from every angle. Consider two camera positions:

- 📷 **From one position**, the players look too close together, so it is hard to tell them apart.
- 📷 **From another position**, the players look clearly separated, and the important details come through.

The photographer must find **the best angle** to capture the essence of a 3D scene in 2D.

**PCA works with a similar idea:**

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Original data** | Higher dimensions |
| **Transformed data** | Lower dimensions |
| **Objective** | Preserve the important characteristics of the original data |

Keep this photographer in mind. We will meet him again at the end.

## ⚡ Why Bother? Two Benefits

> 💭 *"What do I actually gain from PCA?"*

### Benefit 1: Faster execution of machine learning algorithms

Your dataset has **100 features**, and the algorithm must process all of them during training. After PCA reduces them to **10**, the algorithm has much less data to process. As a result:

- the algorithm can **execute faster**
- **computational requirements** are reduced
- the model can **retain similar performance**, because PCA tries to preserve the important information

### Benefit 2: Data visualization

Humans can easily visualise data in **two or three dimensions**. Visualising 10, 50 or 100 dimensions is not straightforward.

PCA can reduce high-dimensional data to **two or three dimensions**, so you can finally draw graphs. The instructor mentions a dataset with **784 columns** that he plans to reduce to **three** dimensions to visualise it.

## 🏠 A Detour Through House Prices: Feature Selection

Before explaining how PCA extracts features, the instructor first explains **feature selection**, because both are dimensionality-reduction techniques.

Consider this house price dataset:

| Number of rooms | Grocery shops nearby | House price |
| --------------- | -------------------- | ----------- |
| 2 | 5 | 30 lakhs |
| 3 | 4 | 45 lakhs |
| 4 | 6 | 60 lakhs |
| 5 | 5 | 80 lakhs |

- **Number of rooms** is an input feature.
- **Grocery shops nearby** is another input feature.
- **House price** is the output.

Suppose you must remove one input feature.

> 💭 *"Which one should I remove?"*

The instructor's answer: **number of rooms matters more** for predicting house price than the number of grocery shops nearby. So we **keep rooms** and **remove grocery shops**. That is **feature selection**.

### How do we pick the important feature mathematically?

Draw a scatter plot:

- **X-axis:** number of rooms
- **Y-axis:** number of grocery shops

You notice that rooms **vary a lot**, whereas grocery shops **do not vary much**.

Now **project all points onto the X-axis**. The distance between the first and last projected points shows how **spread out** the data is along that axis. Do the same for the Y-axis:

- **X-axis:** greater spread
- **Y-axis:** smaller spread

So the instructor's rule is:

> **When deciding between features, the feature with greater variance can be selected.**

### 🔍 Sanity Check: a quick calculation of mine

*(My addition, using the table above and the variance formula that appears later in this chapter.)*

| Feature | Values | Mean | Squared differences from mean | Variance |
| --- | --- | --: | --- | --: |
| Rooms | 2, 3, 4, 5 | 3.5 | 2.25, 0.25, 0.25, 2.25 (sum 5) | 5 / 4 = **1.25** |
| Grocery shops | 5, 4, 6, 5 | 5 | 0, 1, 1, 0 (sum 2) | 2 / 4 = **0.5** |

Rooms has the greater variance (1.25 vs 0.5), matching the instructor's choice.

## 🚧 Where Feature Selection Gets Stuck

Now the instructor changes the example. The features are:

- **Number of rooms**
- **Number of bathrooms**
- **House price**

Both rooms and bathrooms are important for predicting price. Plot them:

- **X-axis:** number of rooms
- **Y-axis:** number of bathrooms

Both features show a **similar spread**, and as rooms increase, bathrooms **also tend to increase**.

> 💭 *"Now which feature should I remove?"*

You **cannot easily decide**. Both look important and their variances are comparable.

> **Feature selection cannot solve every situation where multiple features appear equally important.**

This is exactly where **feature extraction** enters.

---

## 🧑‍💼 The Broker's Idea: Feature Extraction

Suppose you ask a **real estate broker** which feature to remove. The broker knows the domain and says both matter, so don't remove either. Instead, **create a new feature: the size of the flat.**

Why? Because the size of the flat carries information about **both** the number of rooms and the number of bathrooms.

| Size of flat | House price |
| ------------ | ----------- |
| Small | 30 lakhs |
| Medium | 45 lakhs |
| Large | 60 lakhs |

Two original features became **one new feature**. That is **feature extraction**.

### Selection vs Extraction

| Feature Selection | Feature Extraction |
| --- | --- |
| Selects **existing** features | Creates **new** features from existing features |
| Removes less important columns | Transforms the original features into a new representation |
| Retains selected original columns | Uses newly generated features |
| Common techniques: Filter, Wrapper, Embedded methods | Common techniques: **PCA**, LDA |

The instructor's point: **PCA follows the feature extraction approach.** It takes the existing features, creates a **new set of features**, and then **selects the most important components** from that new representation.

> 💭 *"But the broker used domain knowledge. How does PCA do it without one?"*

With geometry. That is the next step.

---

## 🧭 The Geometry: Rotating the Axes

Back to **rooms** and **bathrooms**. We want to convert two input features into one. Feature selection failed, because their variances were comparable. PCA takes a different approach.

### Step 1: Create a new coordinate system

Initially there are two axes:

- **X-axis:** number of rooms
- **Y-axis:** number of bathrooms

PCA **rotates the coordinate system** to create a new set of axes. The old X and Y are replaced by two new directions:

- **Principal Component 1 (PC1)**
- **Principal Component 2 (PC2)**

The data is now described using these new coordinates.

### Step 2: Find the direction with the greatest variance

After rotating, we examine how spread out the data is along the new axes. One direction captures more variance than the other:

- **PC1** captures **greater** variance
- **PC2** captures **less** variance

### Step 3: Keep the important component

Since PC1 has more variance, we **keep PC1** and **remove PC2**. Two input features became **one**: we reduced the dimensionality **from two to one**.

### ⚠️ An important rule

> **The number of principal components is always equal to the number of original features.**

| Original features | Principal components |
| ----------------: | -------------------: |
| 2 | 2 |
| 5 | 5 |
| 10 | 10 |
| 100 | 100 |

The reduction comes from **selecting** which components to keep.

> **The main idea of PCA: transform the original coordinate system into a new one in which the data has maximum variance along the selected directions.**

## 📊 Wait, What Exactly Is Variance?

We have used the word *variance* many times. The instructor now defines it.

**Variance** is a statistical measure of **how spread out the data points are**.

Take two datasets:

- **Dataset A:** `-5, 0, 5`
- **Dataset B:** `-100, 0, 100`

Both have the same mean:

$$
\frac{-5+0+5}{3}=0 \qquad \frac{-100+0+100}{3}=0
$$

The mean tells you the **central value**, but nothing about the **spread**. Dataset B is clearly far more spread out than Dataset A, and variance is what captures that difference.

### The formula

$$
\sigma^2=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i-\mu)^2}{n}
$$

Where:

- $\sigma^2$ = variance
- $x_i$ = an individual data point
- $\mu$ = the mean of the dataset
- $n$ = total number of data points

### How it is calculated, step by step

1. Calculate the **mean**.
2. Calculate the **difference** between each point and the mean.
3. **Square** each difference.
4. **Add** all the squared differences.
5. **Divide** by the number of data points.

A **higher variance** means the points are **more spread out**.

### Variance and standard deviation

$$
\text{Standard Deviation}=\sqrt{\text{Variance}}
$$

Variance is proportional to spread: **greater variance, greater spread.**

### 🔍 Sanity Check: A and B by the formula

*(My addition, applying the lesson's formula to the lesson's datasets.)*

| | Squared differences from mean 0 | Variance | Standard deviation |
| --- | --- | --: | --: |
| Dataset A | 25, 0, 25 (sum 50) | 50 / 3 ≈ **16.67** | √16.67 ≈ **4.08** |
| Dataset B | 10000, 0, 10000 (sum 20000) | 20000 / 3 ≈ **6666.67** | √6666.67 ≈ **81.65** |

Same mean, wildly different variance, exactly as the instructor says.

### 🧮 Why variance and not mean absolute deviation?

> 💭 *"Why does PCA use variance instead of mean absolute deviation?"*

**Mean absolute deviation** is the average *absolute* distance of points from their mean. The instructor's reason for preferring variance:

- PCA involves an **optimization problem**.
- For that optimization, a **differentiable** function is needed.
- **Variance is differentiable**, while the **absolute value** function used in mean absolute deviation is **not differentiable at zero**.

So PCA uses variance. (That is the reason the instructor gives in this lesson.)

## 🚨 The Moment Something Feels Illegal

Now you hold the picture: PCA **rotates the coordinate system**, and then one axis seems to *shrink* or vanish. That feels wrong.

> 💭 *"This seems illegal. PCA deliberately shrinks the values from one axis in the name of rotating the coordinate system, and pretends that the distances between the values and the axis stay the same!"*

Your objection is valid, and it deserves a proper answer. But there is one misunderstanding hiding inside it:

> **PCA is not shrinking the data. It is changing the coordinate system.**

The apparent shrinking comes from looking at the **projection** of points onto individual axes. To see this clearly, we separate the operations and test each one with real numbers.

### 📍 Two points on a diagonal

- **A = (1, 1)**
- **B = (4, 4)**

Their Euclidean distance is the *actual* distance between them:

$$
d=\sqrt{(4-1)^2+(4-1)^2}=\sqrt{18}=4.243
$$

Remember this number. **4.243** is what we are going to try to break.

### 🔄 Turn the axes by 45°

The new coordinates come from:

$$
PC1=\frac{x+y}{\sqrt{2}} \qquad PC2=\frac{-x+y}{\sqrt{2}}
$$

**For A = (1, 1):**

$$
PC1=\frac{1+1}{\sqrt{2}}=1.414 \qquad PC2=\frac{-1+1}{\sqrt{2}}=0
$$

**For B = (4, 4):**

$$
PC1=\frac{4+4}{\sqrt{2}}=5.657 \qquad PC2=\frac{-4+4}{\sqrt{2}}=0
$$

| Point | Original X | Original Y | PC1 | PC2 |
| ----- | ---------: | ---------: | --: | --: |
| A | 1 | 1 | 1.414 | 0 |
| B | 4 | 4 | 5.657 | 0 |

> 💭 *"So did we shrink anything?"*

Recompute the distance with the new coordinates:

$$
d=\sqrt{(5.657-1.414)^2+(0-0)^2}=\sqrt{4.243^2}
$$

$$
\boxed{d=4.243}
$$

**Exactly the same.** Nothing was shrunk.

- Originally, the 4.243 of distance was **spread across two directions** (x and y).
- After the rotation, **all of it lies along PC1**.
- PC2 is zero because both points sit right on the PC1 direction.

This was a **coordinate transformation, not data compression.**

### 🔍 Sanity Check: an extra test of mine

*(My addition, using only the lesson's numbers.)* A rotation should also keep each point's distance from the origin unchanged:

| Point | Distance from origin before | Distance from origin after |
| ----- | --------------------------- | -------------------------- |
| A | √(1² + 1²) = √2 ≈ 1.414 | √(1.414² + 0²) = 1.414 |
| B | √(4² + 4²) = √32 ≈ 5.657 | √(5.657² + 0²) = 5.657 |

Both match. Rotation is length-preserving, which is why your objection about distances was right.

### ✂️ Then where does the reduction happen?

Until now, we have only **rotated**. We still have **two dimensions**:

```text
(PC1, PC2)
```

Only now does PCA make a *decision*:

- **PC1** contains the variation we want to preserve.
- **PC2** contains **zero** variation in this particular example.

So we **discard PC2**, and the representation changes from:

$$
(PC1,PC2) \;\longrightarrow\; (PC1)
$$

**This is where dimensionality reduction actually happens.** It is a *separate step* after the rotation.

In this example we lose **no information**, because PC2 was already zero. In a real dataset, PC2 usually contains *some* variation, and discarding it means losing some information.

### 🧠 The four operations, kept apart

| Operation | What happens? |
| --- | --- |
| **Rotation** | Changes the coordinate representation. |
| **Projection** | Measures data along a particular direction. |
| **Dimensionality reduction** | Discards selected components. |
| **Information loss** | Occurs when the discarded components contain variation. |

The "illegal" feeling came from blending these four into one. Pulled apart, the rotation is perfectly honest, and the only lossy step is the one where you *choose* to throw components away.

### 📐 A correction worth keeping

The earlier infographic in this lesson reported variance values that were **inconsistent**. The corrected values, for those four points and using **population variance** (the same formula as above), are:

| Quantity | Variance |
| --- | -----: |
| Original X | 1.25 |
| Original Y | 1.25 |
| PC1 | 2.5 |
| PC2 | 0 |

*(The four points belong to the earlier infographic, which is not reproduced here, so I am reporting these values as the lesson states them.)*

One check that does not need the points: rotation only redistributes variance, so the total should stay the same. Before: 1.25 + 1.25 = **2.5**. After: 2.5 + 0 = **2.5**. ✅

The rotation took variance that was split evenly across two axes and **concentrated all of it into PC1**.

The lesson's verdict on your objection: *your intuition was correct. A genuine rotation cannot arbitrarily shrink distances. The distinction between rotating the coordinate system and then discarding dimensions is the key to understanding PCA.*

## 🎯 So What Are We Hunting For?

With that cleared up, you take the next step on your own:

> 💭 *"So we are basically looking for an angle that exposes the variance difference we are looking for, because the default axis position failed to do so..."*

**Exactly.** You have grasped the central geometric intuition behind PCA:

> We look for a **new orientation of the coordinate axes** that exposes the **maximum possible variance** along a particular direction.

Remember why the default axes failed in the rooms-and-bathrooms example: the two features are strongly **correlated**, so the points form a **diagonal pattern**. Both original axes show **similar variance**, and **neither alone captures the full variation**. The real structure runs along the diagonal, between the axes.

### What PCA does about it

PCA searches for a new orientation of the axes. It wants to find:

1. **PC1**: the direction along which the projected data has **maximum variance**.
2. **PC2**: the **perpendicular** direction, which captures the remaining variance.

### The whole PCA pipeline, step by step

1. PCA identifies these directions.
2. It **transforms** the original features into principal components.
3. It **ranks** the components by the amount of variance each one captures.
4. **You choose** how many components to retain.
5. You **discard** the rest.

### ⚠️ One refinement to your sentence

Your phrasing was very close, but the lesson tightens it:

> **PCA does not simply look for an angle where the two axes have *different variances*.**
> **It looks for the direction that *maximizes the variance of the projected data*.**

That is the actual **optimization objective**. *Different variances between axes* is a symptom you might see. *Maximum projected variance along a direction* is the goal PCA is truly chasing.

## 🟢🔴 Why Is Maximum Variance the Right Goal?

> 💭 *"Fine, but why variance? Why not some other target?"*

The instructor answers with a picture. Imagine a dataset with two kinds of points, **green** and **red**, and a model that must tell them apart. Now we project the points into a lower-dimensional space.

### Case 1: project onto one axis (the X-axis)

- The green and red points are still **somewhat** separated.
- But the separation is **not very large**.
- Some important information about the **distance between the points is lost**.

### Case 2: project onto another axis (the Y-axis)

- This time the distance between green and red points is **much greater**.
- The two groups are **more clearly separated**.
- This direction **preserves more of the variation** in the data.

So when reducing dimensions, we do not want to lose important information about **how the points are distributed**. That is why PCA **tries to maximize variance**.

### 📸 Back to the photographer

From one angle, the players looked too close together. From another, they were clearly separated, and the photographer chose the angle that captured the scene more clearly.

> **PCA does the same: it chooses the directions that capture the greatest variance in the data.**

The instructor's final takeaway: **the main objective of PCA is to maximize variance when transforming high-dimensional data into lower-dimensional data.**

## 🌀 Deepest Realization

The word "illegal" came from imagining that PCA *forces* distances to shrink. In truth, PCA never breaks the geometry:

> **It first turns the axes without changing anything, then asks which of the new axes are worth keeping.**

The honesty of the method lives in that order: **rotate (lossless) → rank by variance → discard (the only lossy step).**

---

## ✅ Final Takeaway

> *"A rotation changes how you look at the data, never what the data is. PCA turns the axes, like a photographer circling a stadium, until one of them lines up with the greatest spread, and only then does it decide, openly, which directions to let go. Distances are never shrunk by the turning; information is lost only by the discarding."*
