# 🔀 Same Engine, Different Space: How Gradient Boosting Learns to Classify

> *"Regression and classification run the same boosting machine. The only thing that changes is the space the corrections live in."*

You have spent several chapters watching Gradient Boosting do regression: start with the mean, find the residuals, fit a tree to them, add a small correction, repeat. Then comes a new problem, **student placement**: will a student get placed (`1`) or not (`0`)? The output is no longer a salary. It is a class.

So you might be asking yourself:

> *"Do I need a completely different algorithm now? Trees that vote? A new kind of residual? Where does a probability even fit into 'add a correction to the prediction'?"*

The surprising answer is that **you don't need a new algorithm.** The stage-by-stage idea is the same. The major change is the **loss function**, and therefore what the residual represents, plus one conversion step that keeps everything inside the limits of classification.

This chapter is in two parts:

* **Part 1: The Concept.** Why classification needs log-odds, what the pseudo-residual is, why the trees are *regression* trees, and how a new point gets predicted.
* **Part 2: The Worked Example.** An 8-student placement table, followed by hand all the way to a prediction for a brand-new student.

---

# 📘 Part 1: The Concept

## 🧱 The Skeleton Is the Same

Gradient Boosting builds models sequentially, and combines them additively:

```text
Model 0 → makes errors → Tree 1 learns those errors → Tree 2 learns the remaining errors → ...
```

With three stages, the combined model is:

$$
F(x)=F_0(x)+F_1(x)+F_2(x)
$$

where:

* $F_0$ is the initial simple model
* $F_1$ is the first tree, correcting the initial model
* $F_2$ is the second tree, correcting the combined model

The data in our example: inputs such as **CGPA** and **IQ**, and an output of `1` (placement) or `0` (no placement).

---

## ⚖️ What Changes From Regression

| | Regression | Classification |
|---|---|---|
| Loss function | Squared error | **Log loss** |
| Starting model | Mean of $y$ | **Log-odds** of the class proportion |
| What gets corrected | The prediction | The **log-odds score** |
| Residual | $y-\hat y$ | $y - p$ (actual class minus predicted **probability**) |
| Extra step | none | **Sigmoid** to convert score → probability |

Conceptually:

```text
Regression:       Prediction → Residual = y − ŷ
Classification:   Predicted probability → error based on log loss
```

The pseudo-residual ends up as:

$$
r_i = y_i - p_i
$$

where $y_i$ is the actual class (0 or 1) and $p_i$ is the **current predicted probability of class 1**.

---

## 🟰 Stage 0: The Starting Model Uses Log-Odds

Regression began with the mean. Classification begins with something similar: the **class distribution**, converted to **log-odds**.

Suppose a dataset has:

```text
5 students who get placement
3 students who don't
```

Then:

$$
p=\frac{5}{8}=0.625
$$

The odds are:

$$
\frac{p}{1-p}=\frac{0.625}{0.375}\approx 1.667
$$

And the starting score is the **log-odds** (natural log):

$$
F_0=\log\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right)=\log(1.667)\approx 0.511
$$

---

## 🤔 Why Log-Odds? Why Not Just Add Probabilities?

> *"The corrections are being added together. Why not add probability corrections directly?"*

Because probabilities are awkward to add. Adding can easily push a value out of bounds:

$$
0.8+0.5=1.3
$$

A probability cannot be 1.3.

**Log-odds**, however, can range over the whole number line:

$$
-\infty \rightarrow +\infty
$$

So Gradient Boosting can keep doing

$$
F_0+F_1+F_2+\cdots
$$

safely, and **only at the end** convert the resulting score back to a probability. That is the central trick of the classification version.

---

## 🧊 What Does the First Model Predict?

The first model does **not** look at CGPA or IQ. So every student gets exactly the same probability. With $p=0.625$:

| Student | CGPA | IQ  | Initial probability |
| ------- | ---: | --: | ------------------: |
| A       |  8.2 | 120 |               0.625 |
| B       |  6.5 |  90 |               0.625 |
| C       |  9.1 | 140 |               0.625 |
| D       |  5.7 |  80 |               0.625 |

That is a terrible classifier, but it is a starting point.

---

## 🧮 The Pseudo-Residual: How Wrong Was Model 0?

For each row:

$$
r_i = y_i - p_i
$$

**Student A** (actual $y=1$, prediction $p=0.625$):

$$
r = 1-0.625 = +0.375
$$

A positive residual means the model predicted **too little**.

**Student B** (actual $y=0$, prediction $p=0.625$):

$$
r = 0-0.625 = -0.625
$$

A negative residual means the model predicted **too much**.

| Student | Actual $y$ | Current $p$ | Residual $y-p$ |
| ------- | ---------: | ----------: | -------------: |
| A       |          1 |       0.625 |         +0.375 |
| B       |          0 |       0.625 |         -0.625 |
| C       |          1 |       0.625 |         +0.375 |
| D       |          0 |       0.625 |         -0.625 |

---

## 🧭 Why Is $y-p$ the "Gradient"?

> *"For regression the residual came from squared error. Where does $y-p$ come from?"*

It is the **negative gradient of the log loss** with respect to the score $F$ (not the probability). Here is the short derivation. The log loss for one row, written in terms of the score $F$, where $p=\sigma(F)=\dfrac{1}{1+e^{-F}}$, is:

$$
L = -\big[y\log p + (1-y)\log(1-p)\big] = -yF + \log\left(1+e^{F}\right)
$$

Differentiate with respect to $F$:

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial F} = -y + \frac{e^{F}}{1+e^{F}} = p - y
$$

So the **negative gradient** is:

$$
-\frac{\partial L}{\partial F} = y - p
$$

That is exactly the pseudo-residual. The word *pseudo* is there because, unlike regression, it is not "actual minus prediction" in the same units. It is a probability difference being used to steer a log-odds score.

(Derived by hand in this chapter; the scikit-learn documentation confirms the general principle that the trees are fit on the negative gradient of the loss, as noted in the fact-check section.)

---

## 🌳 The Surprise: The Trees Are *Regression* Trees

The second model is a decision tree, but **not a classification tree**. It is a **regression tree**.

> *"But this is a classification problem. Why would the tree be a regression tree?"*

Because the target the tree learns is no longer a class label. It is a continuous residual:

$$
0.375,\;-0.625,\;0.042,\;\ldots
$$

Those aren't classes. So:

$$
\boxed{\text{Classification problem} \rightarrow \text{Regression trees inside Gradient Boosting}}
$$

The tree is also a **weak learner**, a small tree, not a huge fully grown one.

What it learns:

```text
CGPA + IQ
    ↓
Regression Tree
    ↓
Predict residual/error
```

It looks for patterns such as *"students with this CGPA/IQ combination are being under-predicted"* or *"students in this region are being over-predicted."* The inputs stay the original features, $X=(\text{CGPA},\text{IQ})$. Only the **target** changes, from $y$ to $r=y-p$.

---

## 🍃 What Value Does Each Leaf Hold? The $\gamma$ Formula

In regression, each leaf simply held the **mean residual**. In classification, a leaf needs a correction **in log-odds units**, and the plain mean of residuals would be in the wrong units.

For each leaf, the correction is:

$$
\boxed{
\gamma=\frac{\sum r_i}{\sum p_i(1-p_i)}
}
$$

where the sums run over the observations that landed in that leaf, $r_i$ are their residuals, and $p_i$ are their previous probabilities.

> *"Why is there a $p(1-p)$ in the denominator?"*

That term is the **second derivative** of the log loss with respect to $F$ (it measures how sharply the loss curves). Dividing the first-derivative sum by it is a Newton-style step that converts a probability-sized residual into a sensible **log-odds** correction. Example outputs of this calculation look like:

```text
Leaf 1 → γ = +1.6
Leaf 2 → γ = -2.1
Leaf 3 → γ = +0.7
```

These are **not probabilities**. They are corrections in log-odds space. That distinction matters enormously: values like 0.375 or 0.625 are probabilities and must not simply be added to the initial log-odds.

(The exact leaf formula in the original lesson's transcript was garbled by speech-to-text, so the formula above is the standard quantity that the lesson's surrounding calculation corresponds to, not a verbatim transcription.)

---

## ➕ Combine, Then Convert Back

After Tree 1, the combined score is:

$$
F(x)=F_0(x)+F_1(x)
$$

Suppose $F_0=0.511$ and a student's leaf gives $F_1=1.6$ (ignoring the learning rate for a moment):

$$
F(x)=0.511+1.6=2.111
$$

This is still **not a probability**. It is a log-odds score. Convert it with the **sigmoid**:

$$
\boxed{p=\frac{1}{1+e^{-F(x)}}}
$$

$$
p=\frac{1}{1+e^{-2.111}}\approx 0.892
$$

So the combined model now says roughly **89.2%** probability of placement, already far more informative than the original constant.

---

## 🐢 The Learning Rate Works Exactly as Before

Instead of adding the whole correction:

$$
F_{\text{new}}=F_{\text{old}}+F_1(x)
$$

we add a fraction:

$$
\boxed{F_{\text{new}}=F_{\text{old}}+\eta\,F_1(x)}
$$

With $\eta=0.1$, a tree prediction of $1.6$ contributes only $0.1\times1.6=0.16$. The improvement becomes gradual, rather than letting one tree make a gigantic change.

---

## 🔁 Residuals Are Recalculated After Every Stage

We are not finished after Tree 1, because the combined model can still be wrong. So we recompute:

$$
r_i=y_i-p_i
$$

where $p_i$ is now the probability from the **combined** model:

```text
Before Tree 1:   r = y − p₀
After  Tree 1:   r = y − p₁
```

Tree 2 is trained on this **new** residual, not the original one. Then:

$$
F(x)=F_0(x)+\eta F_1(x)+\eta F_2(x)+\cdots
$$

and the error moves toward zero as stages accumulate.

---

## 📦 The Full Classification Formula

$$
\boxed{F(x)=F_0(x)+\eta F_1(x)+\eta F_2(x)+\cdots}
$$

$$
\boxed{p(x)=\sigma(F(x))=\frac{1}{1+e^{-F(x)}}}
$$

$$
\hat y=
\begin{cases}
1 & p\ge 0.5\\
0 & p<0.5
\end{cases}
$$

---

## 🆕 How a Brand-New Student Is Predicted

Suppose a new student arrives with $\text{CGPA}=7.2$ and $\text{IQ}=100$. The model does not retrain and does not compare this student against every existing student. Instead:

1. **Pass through the initial model.** Get $F_0$.
2. **Pass through Tree 1.** The tree asks its learned split questions, lands in a leaf, and that leaf gives a correction $F_1(x)$.
3. **Pass through Tree 2.** It lands in another leaf and gives another correction $F_2(x)$.
4. **Continue through every tree.**
5. **Combine:** $F(x)=F_0+\eta F_1(x)+\eta F_2(x)+\cdots$
6. **Convert to probability:** $p=\dfrac{1}{1+e^{-F(x)}}$
7. **Turn probability into a class.** For example $p=0.78$ gives **Placement = YES**.

As in regression, **no residual is computed at prediction time**, because the true label is unknown.

---

## 🗳️ The Subtle Point: Trees Don't Vote

A normal classification tree might say *"Placement"* or *"No placement."* Inside Gradient Boosting that is **not** what is happening:

```text
NOT THIS:
Tree 1 → YES
Tree 2 → NO
Tree 3 → YES      (majority vote)
```

```text
THIS:
Baseline log-odds
   +
Correction 1
   +
Correction 2
   +
Correction 3
   +
...
   ↓
Final log-odds
   ↓
Sigmoid
   ↓
Probability
   ↓
Class
```

Each tree says *"move the current log-odds this much up or down,"* not *"here is the class."*

---

## 🌲 vs 🪜 Random Forest

**Random Forest:** trees trained **independently**, then combined or voted.

```text
Tree 1 ──→ prediction
Tree 2 ──→ prediction
Tree 3 ──→ prediction
Tree 4 ──→ prediction
              ↓
        Combine / Vote
```

**Gradient Boosting:** trees trained **sequentially**.

```text
Model 0 → errors → Tree 1 → new errors → Tree 2 → new errors → Tree 3 → ...
```

---

## 🏔️ The Geometry Intuition

Take two input features $x_1, x_2$ and a binary output $y\in\{0,1\}$. Picture the data in 3D, with the class as the third dimension: class-0 points sit around one level, class-1 points around another.

```text
Initial model
     ↓
almost flat prediction surface
     ↓
Tree 1 adjusts it
     ↓
more useful surface
     ↓
Tree 2 adjusts it
     ↓
more flexible surface
     ↓
Tree 3 adjusts it
     ↓
better separation between classes
```

In plots, the decision boundary becomes progressively more flexible as trees are added.

---

## 🔗 The Insight Worth Keeping: Regression Plus a Conversion

A useful way to hold all of this:

> *"This is just regression, but the conversion step is what keeps everything inside the limits of classification."*

That is close, with one refinement that is worth getting right:

* The **additive boosting itself happens in log-odds space**, where scores can move freely up and down.
* The **sigmoid** then maps that unconstrained score back to a valid probability in $[0,1]$.

So the conversion is not a cosmetic last step. It is part of the architecture:

$$
\boxed{\text{Regression-like boosting} + \text{Log-odds/probability transformation} = \text{Gradient Boosting Classification}}
$$

Side by side:

```text
Regression                           Classification
Start with mean                      Start with log-odds
      ↓                                    ↓
calculate residuals                  convert to probability
      ↓                                    ↓
fit regression tree                  calculate pseudo-residuals
      ↓                                    ↓
add correction                       fit regression tree
      ↓                                    ↓
repeat                               add correction to log-odds
                                           ↓
                                     convert to probability
                                           ↓
                                     repeat
```

---

## 🧠 The Chain to Memorize

$$
\boxed{
\text{Initial log-odds} \rightarrow \text{Probability} \rightarrow \text{Residual} \rightarrow \text{Regression Tree} \rightarrow \text{Leaf Correction} \rightarrow \text{Add to log-odds} \rightarrow \text{Probability} \rightarrow \text{Repeat}
}
$$

In plain English: *start with a crude probability, find where it is wrong, train a small regression tree to model those errors, add its correction to the current log-odds, convert back to probability, and repeat.*

```text
                 TRAINING
                    │
                    ▼
          Initial class probability
                    │
                    ▼
        Convert to log-odds F₀
                    │
                    ▼
           Predict every row
                    │
                    ▼
      Calculate pseudo-residuals
             r = y − p
                    │
                    ▼
       Train WEAK REGRESSION TREE
          on X → residuals
                    │
                    ▼
       Calculate leaf corrections γ
                    │
                    ▼
         Add learning-rate-scaled
              corrections
                    │
                    ▼
          New combined log-odds
                    │
                    ▼
              Sigmoid
                    │
                    ▼
           New probabilities
                    │
                    ▼
        Calculate new residuals
                    │
                    ▼
              Repeat...
```

# 🧪 Part 2: The Worked Example

Time to run every step of Part 1 on real numbers.

## 📋 The Data

| Row | CGPA |  IQ | Actual $y$ |
| --: | ---: | --: | ---------: |
|   1 | 6.82 | 118 |          0 |
|   2 | 6.36 | 125 |          1 |
|   3 | 5.39 |  99 |          1 |
|   4 | 5.50 | 106 |          1 |
|   5 | 6.39 | 148 |          0 |
|   6 | 9.13 | 148 |          1 |
|   7 | 7.17 | 147 |          0 |
|   8 | 7.72 |  72 |          0 |

There are four 1s (rows 2, 3, 4, 6) and four 0s (rows 1, 5, 7, 8). We use $\eta=0.1$.

---

## 🟰 Model 0: The Starting Model

The starting probability is the class proportion:

$$
p_0=\frac{4}{8}=0.5
$$

Stored in log-odds space:

$$
F_0=\log\left(\frac{0.5}{0.5}\right)=\log(1)=0
$$

```text
Initial score       F₀ = 0
Initial probability    = sigmoid(0) = 0.5
```

Every student starts at **0.5**.

---

## 🧮 Residuals: What Did Model 0 Get Wrong?

$$
r_i=y_i-p_i
$$

| Row | Actual | Prediction | Residual |
| --: | -----: | ---------: | -------: |
|   1 |      0 |        0.5 |     -0.5 |
|   2 |      1 |        0.5 |     +0.5 |
|   3 |      1 |        0.5 |     +0.5 |
|   4 |      1 |        0.5 |     +0.5 |
|   5 |      0 |        0.5 |     -0.5 |
|   6 |      1 |        0.5 |     +0.5 |
|   7 |      0 |        0.5 |     -0.5 |
|   8 |      0 |        0.5 |     -0.5 |

Now the handoff to the next tree:

```text
Original inputs:         CGPA, IQ
Target for next tree:    residual
```

---

## 🌳 Tree 1 Is a Regression Tree

The overall problem is classification, but the tree's target is now

```text
-0.5, +0.5, +0.5, +0.5, ...
```

not 0 or 1. So Tree 1 learns:

$$
\text{CGPA, IQ} \rightarrow \text{residual}
$$

---

## ✂️ Tree 1 Makes a Split (Illustrative)

To make the mechanics concrete, suppose this weak tree discovers:

```text
                 CGPA <= 6.375?
                  /            \
                YES             NO
                 |               |
              Leaf A          Leaf B
```

Which rows go where?

* **Leaf A** (CGPA ≤ 6.375): rows **2, 3, 4** (CGPA 6.36, 5.39, 5.50)
* **Leaf B** (CGPA > 6.375): rows **1, 5, 6, 7, 8** (CGPA 6.82, 6.39, 9.13, 7.17, 7.72)

(This split is an assumed illustration. I did not fit a model, so I cannot confirm that a real tree would choose it.)

### Computing the leaf corrections $\gamma$

Every row currently has $p=0.5$, so $p(1-p)=0.25$ for each row.

**Leaf A** has residuals $+0.5,+0.5,+0.5$:

$$
\gamma_A=\frac{0.5+0.5+0.5}{3\times0.25}=\frac{1.5}{0.75}=+2.0
$$

**Leaf B** has residuals $-0.5,\,-0.5,\,+0.5,\,-0.5,\,-0.5$ for rows 1, 5, 6, 7, 8:

$$
\gamma_B=\frac{-0.5-0.5+0.5-0.5-0.5}{5\times0.25}=\frac{-1.5}{1.25}=-1.2
$$

These are **not probabilities**. They are corrections to the log-odds score.

---

## ➕ Apply the Learning Rate and Convert

**Row 3** (CGPA 5.39, Leaf A):

$$
F_1(x)=0+0.1(2.0)=0.2
$$

$$
p=\frac{1}{1+e^{-0.2}}\approx 0.55
$$

Row 3 moves from $0.50 \rightarrow 0.55$. It moved **up**, because this region holds students the original model underestimated.

**Row 1** (CGPA 6.82, Leaf B):

$$
F_1(x)=0+0.1(-1.2)=-0.12
$$

$$
p=\frac{1}{1+e^{0.12}}\approx 0.47
$$

Row 1 moves from $0.50 \rightarrow 0.47$. It moved **down**, because this region was mostly overestimated.

---

## 📊 After Tree 1

| Row | Actual | Initial $p$ | Tree correction | New $p$ |
| --: | -----: | ----------: | --------------: | ------: |
|   1 |      0 |        0.50 |           -0.12 |    0.47 |
|   2 |      1 |        0.50 |           +0.20 |    0.55 |
|   3 |      1 |        0.50 |           +0.20 |    0.55 |
|   4 |      1 |        0.50 |           +0.20 |    0.55 |
|   5 |      0 |        0.50 |           -0.12 |    0.47 |
|   6 |      1 |        0.50 |           -0.12 |    0.47 |
|   7 |      0 |        0.50 |           -0.12 |    0.47 |
|   8 |      0 |        0.50 |           -0.12 |    0.47 |

Notice that Tree 1 did **not** say *"Row 3 = class 1, Row 1 = class 0."* It said:

```text
Row 3 → add +0.20 to the model score
Row 1 → add −0.12 to the model score
```

That is the boosting mindset.

---

## 😬 Tree 1 Is Still Wrong

Take **Row 6**: CGPA 9.13, IQ 148, actual $=1$. After Tree 1 it sits at $p\approx0.47$, but the truth is $1$. Its new residual:

$$
r=1-0.47\approx +0.53
$$

Gradient Boosting's answer: *"Fine. Tree 1 didn't fix this completely. Give the remaining error to another tree."*

---

## 🌳 Tree 2: A New Target

Recompute the residual for every row using the **updated** probabilities. Tree 2 is trained on `CGPA, IQ → NEW residual`, **not** the original residual:

```text
Model 0 → residuals₁ → Tree 1 → new probability → residuals₂ → Tree 2
```

---

## 🧾 Combining Two Stages

At this point:

$$
F(x)=F_0(x)+\eta F_1(x)+\eta F_2(x)
$$

For one particular row, suppose $F_0=0$, Tree 1 contributes $0.2$ and Tree 2 contributes $0.15$ (the Tree 2 value is illustrative):

$$
F(x)=0+0.2+0.15=0.35
$$

$$
p=\frac{1}{1+e^{-0.35}}\approx 0.587
$$

The final probability is about **58.7%**.

---

## 🆕 A New Student Arrives

A completely new student: $\boxed{\text{CGPA}=7.2,\ \text{IQ}=100}$.

### Step 1: Model 0

$$
F_0=0
$$

### Step 2: Tree 1

The learned question is $\text{CGPA}\le6.375$?

$$
7.2>6.375
$$

```text
New student → Tree 1 → Right leaf (Leaf B) → correction = −1.2
```

With learning rate 0.1:

$$
-1.2\times0.1=-0.12
$$

The score is now $F=-0.12$, and $p\approx0.47$.

### Step 3: The SAME student goes into Tree 2, Tree 3, ...

This is the part people often miss: the new student does not stop after Tree 1. It travels through every tree, and each tree asks its own split questions:

```text
             Tree 1
               ↓
          Leaf → −0.12
               ↓
             Tree 2
               ↓
          Leaf → +0.08
               ↓
             Tree 3
               ↓
          Leaf → +0.05
```

(These per-tree values are already learning-rate-scaled contributions. The Tree 2 and Tree 3 values are **illustrative**, not from trees I trained.)

$$
F(x)=0-0.12+0.08+0.05=0.01
$$

### Step 4: Sigmoid

$$
p=\sigma(0.01)\approx 0.5025
$$

$$
\boxed{P(\text{placement})\approx 50.25\%}
$$

Since $0.5025\ge0.5$, the predicted class would be `1`, but only barely. The actual number would depend on the exact trees and leaf values learned during training. The point here is the **mechanism**, not a result from a trained model.

---

## 🔀 Regression vs Classification, Same New-Point Logic

**Regression**

```text
Initial prediction → Residual → Regression Tree → Correction → Add → New prediction → Repeat
```

Example ledger:

$$
100+8-3+2=\boxed{107}
$$

**Classification**

```text
Initial log-odds → Probability → Residual → Regression Tree → Log-odds correction → Add → New probability → Repeat
```

Example ledger:

$$
0+0.2-0.12+0.08=\boxed{0.16}, \qquad \sigma(0.16)\approx 0.54 \Rightarrow \boxed{54\%}
$$

The cleanest summary:

* **In regression**, each tree says *"add this much to the prediction."*
* **In classification**, each tree says *"move the current log-odds this much up or down."*

And at the end:

$$
\boxed{\text{final log-odds} \rightarrow \text{sigmoid} \rightarrow \text{probability} \rightarrow \text{class}}
$$

When a new value arrives:

$$
\boxed{X_{\text{new}} \rightarrow T_1 \rightarrow T_2 \rightarrow T_3 \rightarrow\cdots \rightarrow \text{sum corrections} \rightarrow \text{sigmoid} \rightarrow \text{prediction}}
$$

Every tree participates in predicting the new point.

6. **Leaf B:** $-1.5/1.25=-1.2$ ✔
7. **Row 3:** $\sigma(0.2)\approx0.55$ ✔
8. **Row 1:** $\sigma(-0.12)\approx0.47$ ✔
9. **Row 6 residual:** $1-0.47=0.53$ ✔
10. **Two-stage example:** $\sigma(0.35)\approx0.587$ ✔
11. **New student:** $0-0.12+0.08+0.05=0.01$ and $\sigma(0.01)\approx0.5025$ ✔
12. **Side-by-side ledgers:** $100+8-3+2=107$; $0+0.2-0.12+0.08=0.16$ and $\sigma(0.16)\approx0.54$ ✔

## 🌉 ML Bridge

The classification version maps directly onto scikit-learn's `GradientBoostingClassifier`:

| Concept in this chapter | Library behavior |
|---|---|
| Log loss | `loss='log_loss'` (default) |
| Regression trees fit on the negative gradient | Documented behavior of the classifier |
| Learning rate $\eta$ | `learning_rate` (default 0.1) |
| Tree size | `max_depth` (default 3) |

Two practical consequences. First, `predict_proba` gives you the sigmoid output, while `predict` applies the 0.5-style cutoff. Second, because every tree is a regression tree, a classification model's trees can be inspected as regression trees with numeric leaf values, which are log-odds corrections.

---

## 🌀 Deepest Realization

Gradient Boosting never changes **what** it is doing, only **where** it does it. Corrections are always "move the current score toward lower loss." In regression the score *is* the prediction. In classification the score is a **log-odds** value that only becomes a prediction after the sigmoid.

> **Boosting happens in a space where numbers can add freely. Probability is just the view you take of that space at the end.**

---

## ✅ Final Takeaway

> **Gradient Boosting classification is the regression machine running in log-odds space. It starts from the class log-odds, turns scores into probabilities with the sigmoid, computes the pseudo-residual $y-p$, trains a small regression tree on it, and adds a learning-rate-scaled leaf correction to the log-odds. Trees never vote: a new point travels through every tree, the corrections are summed on top of the starting score, and only then does the sigmoid turn the total into a probability and a class.**
