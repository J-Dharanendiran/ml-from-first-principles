# 🎯 The Five Promises: What Linear Regression Quietly Assumes About You

> *"Every model that fits your data isn't necessarily a model you can trust."*

You've derived the Normal Equation. You've walked gradient descent down the bowl of the loss surface, step by careful step. You've compared both paths and watched them land in the same place. By now, fitting a line through data feels almost mechanical — hand it $X$ and $Y$, and it hands you back $\beta_0, \beta_1, \dots, \beta_n$.

But here's the question that should be nagging at you:

> *Just because the model fits — does that mean you can trust what it's telling you?*

That question is the whole chapter. Because Linear Regression will **always** give you an answer. It doesn't check whether the answer means anything. It just does the algebra. The checking — that part is on you. And that's exactly what these five assumptions are: a checklist for whether the promise your model made is one it can actually keep.

Think back to your Stroke Prediction model for a second. If you're feeding it patient features and it spits out a risk number, you'd want to know — is this number *earned*, or is it just arithmetic wearing a lab coat? That's the difference an assumption check makes.

---

## Why do assumptions even exist?

Strip the model down to its skeleton:

$$
Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_1 + \beta_2 X_2 + \cdots + \beta_n X_n + \epsilon
$$

- $Y$ → the target
- $X$ → the features
- $\beta$ → the coefficients
- $\epsilon$ → the leftover noise

The model is making a quiet bet: *"Everything I can't explain with a straight line is just random noise."*

The five assumptions are simply five different ways of testing that bet. If the leftover error ($\epsilon$) turns out to have a *pattern* — a shape, a trend, a relationship with something else — the model lost its own bet. It missed something real and mislabeled it as randomness.

---

## 1️⃣ The Straight-Line Promise: Linear Relationship

> *"Does the output actually move in a straight line with the input?"*

Picture house size against price:

```
Price
 ^
 |                        *
 |                    *
 |                *
 |            *
 |        *
 |    *
 | *
 +---------------------------->
           Size
```

That's the shape the model is built for. Now picture this instead:

```
Price
 ^
 |                           *
 |                     *
 |                *
 |          *
 |     *
 | *
 +---------------------------->
      x² relationship
```

Same idea — bigger house, more expensive — but the *rate* is accelerating. And here's the quiet catch: Linear Regression's equation only has room for one power of $X$. No $X^2$, no $\log(X)$, no $e^X$. Just $X$. So when the true relationship curves, the model doesn't bend to follow it — it forces a straight line through a curve anyway, and the gap between the line and the data becomes your error.

**With multiple features**, this promise has to hold *individually* for each one. Size, bedrooms, age, distance — each needs its own roughly-straight relationship with price. Not perfect. Just reasonably straight.

**How you'd actually check it:** a scatter plot of each feature against the target.

```python
sns.scatterplot(x=df["Size"], y=df["Price"])
```

A clean diagonal → the promise holds. A curve, a skew, a shapeless scatter → it doesn't, and you're looking at polynomial features, log transforms, or a model that isn't linear at all — decision trees, random forest, gradient boosting.

---

## 2️⃣ The Independence Promise: No Multicollinearity

This is the one that trips people up first — not because it's hard, but because it's *sneaky*. The model still fits fine. The predictions can even look fine. It's the coefficients that quietly stop meaning anything.

> *"Are my input features secretly the same information wearing two different names?"*

Height in centimeters. Height in meters. Height in feet. Three columns, one fact. Or: age and birth year — move one, and the other moves in lockstep. There's no independent information left to separate.

Here's why that breaks things. The model's equation:

$$
Y = \beta_1 X_1 + \beta_2 X_2
$$

is trying to answer: *"How much does $Y$ change from $X_1$ alone, holding $X_2$ perfectly still?"* But if $X_1$ and $X_2$ always move together, "holding $X_2$ still" is a request the data can never satisfy. The model has no way to cleanly credit one variable over the other.

> *Two scientists worked on the same paper — an atomic physicist and a chemist. Easy to separate their contributions; their skills don't overlap. But now imagine both scientists have identical training, identical skills. Your manager asks: "who contributed 70%?" Nobody can honestly answer. That's multicollinearity, sitting inside your coefficients.*

**Symptom:** retrain the same model tomorrow and watch a coefficient swing from 5200 to 1300. Nothing about your data changed. The model just can't tell the correlated features apart, so it distributes credit almost arbitrarily between them.

**How you'd check it:**

Correlation heatmap first —

| Feature | Experience | Age |
|---|---|---|
| Experience | 1 | 0.95 |
| Age | 0.95 | 1 |

0.95 is a loud warning sign. But the more rigorous tool is the **Variance Inflation Factor**:

$$
VIF = \frac{1}{1 - R^2}
$$

where $R^2$ comes from regressing that one feature against *all the others*.

| VIF | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 1 | Perfectly independent |
| 1–5 | Usually fine |
| >5 | Worth investigating |
| >10 | Serious problem |

```python
from statsmodels.stats.outliers_influence import variance_inflation_factor

vif = pd.DataFrame()
vif["Feature"] = X.columns
vif["VIF"] = [
    variance_inflation_factor(X.values, i)
    for i in range(X.shape[1])
]
```

Fix it by dropping one of the redundant features, combining them, reducing dimensions with PCA, or leaning on Ridge Regression, which is built to tolerate this exact problem.

---

## 3️⃣ The Bell-Curve Promise: Normality of Residuals

> *"Do the model's mistakes look random, or do they look like they're hiding a pattern?"*

First, the residual itself — nothing more than actual minus predicted:

| Actual | Prediction | Residual |
|---|---|---|
| 100 | 95 | 5 |
| 150 | 145 | 5 |
| 200 | 205 | -5 |
| 250 | 248 | 2 |
| 300 | 310 | -10 |

Now zoom out and look at *all* the residuals together. A trustworthy model makes many small mistakes, a handful of medium ones, and very few large ones — which naturally stacks into a bell shape:

```
           *
        *     *
      *         *
    *             *
***********************
```

centered near zero. Compare that to residuals that look like this:

```
*************
*
*
*
*
*
```

That's not random error anymore — that's a model with a systematic bias baked in.

**How you'd check it:** a KDE or histogram of the residuals, or a Q-Q plot — where a normal distribution shows up as points hugging the diagonal.

```python
sns.kdeplot(residuals)
stats.probplot(residuals, dist="norm", plot=plt)
```

Worth being honest about scope here: this assumption matters most for statistical *inference* — trusting your confidence intervals and p-values. If your only goal is prediction, a mild departure from normality is often survivable.

---

## 4️⃣ The Equal-Spread Promise: Homoscedasticity

Break the word apart — *homo* (same), *scedasticity* (spread). That's the entire assumption.

> *"Is the model equally wrong everywhere, or is it wildly wrong in some corners and fine in others?"*

Say you're predicting house prices. Small houses miss by about $2,000. Medium houses miss by $2,100. Large houses miss by $1,900. Roughly even — that's homoscedasticity.

Now imagine small houses miss by $500, but large houses miss by $30,000. The model isn't uniformly reliable anymore — it's confidently accurate in one region and quietly falling apart in another.

Visually:

```
Good — uniform cloud          Bad — funnel shape
Residual                      Residual
  *                                 *
 * *                               * *
*  *                              *   *
 * *                             *      *
                                *         *
------------------                ------------------
   Predicted                        Predicted
```

**How you'd check it:** plot predicted values against residuals.

```python
sns.scatterplot(x=y_pred, y=residuals)
```

A funnel shape is the tell. Fixes range from log-transforming the target, to Weighted Least Squares, to Robust Regression, to simply engineering better features that explain away the widening error.

---

## 5️⃣ The No-Memory Promise: No Autocorrelation

> *"Does one error know about the last one?"*

Residuals are supposed to be strangers to each other — each one independent, random noise with no relationship to the error before it.

Compare:

```
Random (good):     3   -2    1    0    4   -1
Trending (bad):     4    5    6    7    8    9
Oscillating (bad):  3    2    1    0   -1   -2   -3
```

If the errors trend upward, or oscillate, or otherwise follow each other, that's not noise — that's a signal the model failed to capture. Something structured is still hiding in the data, and the model mislabeled it as randomness rather than learning it.

**How you'd check it:** plot the residuals in sequence.

```python
plt.plot(residuals)
```

Random noise looks like static. A repeating wave means trouble. The formal version of this check is the **Durbin-Watson statistic**:

```python
from statsmodels.stats.stattools import durbin_watson
durbin_watson(residuals)
```

| Value | Meaning |
|---|---|
| ≈ 2 | No autocorrelation |
| < 2 | Positive autocorrelation |
| > 2 | Negative autocorrelation |

---

## 🧭 Sanity Check

Put all five side by side, and a pattern of your own emerges — each assumption is really asking the same underlying question in a different disguise: *"Is what's left over after my line actually random, or did I miss something?"*

| Assumption | Why it matters | How to check | Common fixes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Linear Relationship | A straight line can only capture linear trends | Scatter plots per feature | Polynomial features, transforms, tree models |
| No Multicollinearity | Correlated features make coefficients unstable | Correlation matrix, VIF | Drop/combine features, PCA, Ridge |
| Normality of Residuals | Supports valid confidence intervals & p-values | Histogram, KDE, Q-Q plot | Transform variables, feature engineering |
| Homoscedasticity | Ensures errors are consistently sized | Residuals vs. predicted plot | Log transform, WLS, Robust Regression |
| No Autocorrelation | Errors shouldn't be predictable from each other | Residual sequence plot, Durbin-Watson | Add missing variables, model the trend |

---

## 🔗 ML Bridge

Every assumption you just walked through lives downstream of the same equation you derived when you built the Normal Equation and watched gradient descent converge. Linear Regression doesn't check any of these five conditions before it fits — it will happily hand you coefficients whether the data honors them or not. The fitting process and the trust-worthiness of the result are two separate questions, and only one of them is answered by minimizing loss.

If you ran this checklist against your Stroke Prediction feature set — age, glucose level, BMI, hypertension flag — this is exactly the audit you'd want before believing what any coefficient is telling you about risk. A high VIF between two of those features wouldn't break the model's predictions, but it would quietly wreck your ability to say *"this feature drives risk more than that one."*

---

## 🌀 Deepest Realization

Here's the part that's easy to miss the first time through: none of these five assumptions are required for the model to *train*. Not one. Ordinary Least Squares will compute a hyperplane through absolute chaos, through curved data, through duplicated features, through errors that trend upward like a staircase — and it will do so without complaint. The algebra doesn't care.

What breaks isn't the fitting. What breaks is the *trust*. A violated assumption doesn't necessarily show up as a bad-looking prediction — it can hide behind a perfectly respectable $R^2$ while the coefficients underneath are unstable, the confidence intervals are fiction, and the model's story about *why* it predicts what it predicts quietly stops being true.

That's the real reason experienced practitioners don't stop the moment the model fits. They go looking for the cracks on purpose — because a model can be simultaneously *accurate* and *dishonest about why*.
