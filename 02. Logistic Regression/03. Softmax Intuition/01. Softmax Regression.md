# 🥧 The Pie That Always Adds Up to One

> *"You already know how to ask a model one question. This chapter is about teaching it to answer several — without lying to any of them."*

---

You already know binary logistic regression. You've sat with the sigmoid, you've watched a single score curve into a single probability, and you've trusted the number that came out the other end. So when a new problem lands on your desk, the instinct is to reach for the same tool.

The problem looks like this:

| CGPA | IQ | Class      |
| ---: | -: | ---------- |
|  7.1 | 85 | Placed     |
|  6.2 | 80 | Not placed |
|  8.5 | 95 | Opt-out    |

Three outcomes. Mutually exclusive. Every student belongs to exactly one of them. And the question in your head is immediate:

> **"Can't I just reuse logistic regression here?"**

Not quite — and understanding *why not* is the whole story.

## 🔍 Why the old tool won't fit

Binary logistic regression was never built to answer "which of three." It was built to answer one narrower question:

$$
P(y=1\mid x)
$$

and then, because there were only ever two doors in the room, the second probability fell out for free:

$$
P(y=0\mid x)=1-P(y=1\mid x)
$$

That trick — *the second answer is just "everything the first one didn't take"* — only works when there are two doors. The moment you add a third door, subtraction stops being enough. Now you need three separate numbers:

$$
P(y=0\mid x),\quad P(y=1\mid x),\quad P(y=2\mid x)
$$

and they can't just be three unrelated guesses. They have to behave like a *real* distribution:

$$
P_0+P_1+P_2=1
$$

Something like:

$$
P_0=0.20,\quad P_1=0.65,\quad P_2=0.15
$$

That constraint — every slice bounded between 0 and 1, all slices summing to exactly one whole pie — is *precisely* the job Softmax was built to do.

## 🌉 The bridge you didn't expect

Here's the part worth sitting with before touching a single formula:

> **Binary logistic regression is not a different algorithm from Softmax. It's a special case of it.**

Set the number of classes $K=2$ inside Softmax, and the machinery collapses mathematically back into the ordinary sigmoid. So the honest way to think about what's coming isn't *"I finished logistic regression, now here's something unrelated."* It's:

> *Softmax is logistic regression, refusing to stop at two doors.*

The skeleton never changes:

$$
X \rightarrow \text{linear scores} \rightarrow \text{probabilities} \rightarrow \text{class}
$$

Only the middle step gets more ambitious.

## 🎯 Before probabilities: the raw scores

In binary logistic regression, you calculated one number:

$$
z=w_1x_1+w_2x_2+b
$$

then squeezed it through sigmoid. For multiclass classification, you don't calculate *one* score anymore — you calculate **one score per class**. For three classes, that's three separate linear equations running in parallel:

$$
z_1=w_{11}x_1+w_{21}x_2+b_1
$$

$$
z_2=w_{12}x_1+w_{22}x_2+b_2
$$

$$
z_3=w_{13}x_1+w_{23}x_2+b_3
$$

A single student might produce something like:

$$
[z_1,\ z_2,\ z_3]=[2.5,\ 1.0,\ -0.5]
$$

These raw numbers are called **logits**, and here's the detail that trips people up the first time they see it:

> *"Wait — shouldn't these add up to something meaningful, like 1?"*

They don't have to. Logits aren't probabilities yet. They can be negative, they can sum to anything, they carry no promise at all. They're just *scores* — a rough measure of how much each class likes this particular student, before anything has been normalized.

## ✨ Where the exponential earns its place

Softmax is the machine that turns those raw, unconstrained scores into a proper distribution:

$$
\boxed{P_k=\frac{e^{z_k}}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}e^{z_j}}}
$$

For three classes, that expands into:

$$
P_1=\frac{e^{z_1}}{e^{z_1}+e^{z_2}+e^{z_3}},\quad
P_2=\frac{e^{z_2}}{e^{z_1}+e^{z_2}+e^{z_3}},\quad
P_3=\frac{e^{z_3}}{e^{z_1}+e^{z_2}+e^{z_3}}
$$

But *why the exponential*, specifically? Not just because it looks mathematically respectable.

$e^z$ is **always positive**, for any real $z$ — even $e^{-10}$ is still a small positive number. That matters, because a probability can never be negative, and a raw logit could easily be. But the exponential does something more useful than just fixing signs: it **magnifies differences**. Take:

$$
z_1=3,\quad z_2=1,\quad z_3=0
$$

Exponentiate each:

$$
e^3\approx20.09,\quad e^1\approx2.72,\quad e^0=1
$$

Notice how the gap between the scores got *louder*. A logit that was only somewhat larger becomes dramatically more dominant once exponentiated. Softmax doesn't just preserve the ranking of the raw scores — it sharpens it into something closer to a confident decision.

## ➗ Why divide by the sum

Exponentiating alone isn't a probability distribution — the numbers don't sum to 1 yet. That's the job of the denominator. Sum the exponentiated scores:

$$
20.09+2.72+1=23.81
$$

then divide each piece by that total:

$$
P_1=\frac{20.09}{23.81}\approx0.844,\quad
P_2=\frac{2.72}{23.81}\approx0.114,\quad
P_3=\frac{1}{23.81}\approx0.042
$$

Check the sum:

$$
0.844+0.114+0.042\approx1
$$

The denominator is a **normalization factor** — it's the thing that forces every Softmax output to obey:

$$
\boxed{\sum_{k=1}^{K}P_k=1}
$$

That's the whole trick. Three unruly, unbounded scores walk in; one well-behaved probability distribution walks out.

## 🧩 What the model is actually doing, end to end

Put it together for a new student with features $x=[\text{CGPA}, \text{IQ}]$:

$$
z_1=w_{11}\text{CGPA}+w_{21}\text{IQ}+b_1
$$

$$
z_2=w_{12}\text{CGPA}+w_{22}\text{IQ}+b_2
$$

$$
z_3=w_{13}\text{CGPA}+w_{23}\text{IQ}+b_3
$$

Then:

$$
[z_1,z_2,z_3]\ \xrightarrow{\text{Softmax}}\ [P_1,P_2,P_3]
$$

And the model finally commits to an answer:

$$
\boxed{\arg\max(P_1,P_2,P_3)}
$$

Whichever class holds the highest probability *becomes* the prediction. This is worth being precise about, because it's a subtlety people gloss over:

> **Softmax itself does not choose a class.** It only produces the distribution. A separate step — `argmax` — is what turns "here are three probabilities" into "here is one answer."

$$
\text{Linear scores} \rightarrow \text{Softmax probabilities} \rightarrow \text{argmax decision}
$$

## 🧠 The intuitive picture first: three models pretending to be one

Before the real implementation, there's a way of *thinking* about Softmax that makes it feel less alien — building three separate binary classifiers, one per class.

Take the same three students, one-hot encoded:

| Class      | Placed | Not Placed | Opt-out |
| ---------- | -----: | ---------: | ------: |
| Placed     |      1 |          0 |       0 |
| Not Placed |      0 |          1 |       0 |
| Opt-out    |      0 |          0 |       1 |

Now imagine three separate binary questions:

- **Model 1** asks: *"Placed, or everything else?"* → target $[1,0,0]$
- **Model 2** asks: *"Not placed, or everything else?"* → target $[0,1,0]$
- **Model 3** asks: *"Opt-out, or everything else?"* → target $[0,0,1]$

Each of those is ordinary binary logistic regression, wearing a different hat. This is the **one-vs-rest** intuition, and it's genuinely useful for building a mental model — but it isn't what actually gets implemented.

With 2 input features and 3 classes, each individual classifier needs 3 parameters ($w_1, w_2, b$). Three classifiers means:

$$
3\times3=9\ \text{parameters}
$$

arranged conceptually as something like:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
w_{11} & w_{12} & w_{13}\\
w_{21} & w_{22} & w_{23}\\
b_1 & b_2 & b_3
\end{bmatrix}
$$

— each *column* belonging to one class.

## ⚠️ But that's not what actually gets trained

Here's the twist the intuitive picture hides:

> *"So does training a multiclass model mean training three completely independent models?"*

No — and this distinction matters. Training three fully separate binary classifiers becomes wasteful fast: more classes, more samples, more features, and you're multiplying independent training runs that don't share any information with each other. The real approach builds **one unified model**, with **one shared loss function**, optimized jointly. The one-vs-rest picture is the training wheels; Softmax Regression is what happens once you take them off.

## 📉 The loss function that makes it one model, not three

Binary logistic regression used **Binary Cross-Entropy**:

$$
L=-\left[y\log(\hat y)+(1-y)\log(1-\hat y)\right]
$$

The multiclass generalization is **Categorical Cross-Entropy**:

$$
\boxed{L=-\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\sum_{k=1}^{K}y_k^{(i)}\log(\hat y_k^{(i)})}
$$

The double summation looks intimidating the first time you meet it, so take it apart piece by piece.

The **outer sum**, $\sum_{i=1}^{M}$, just means: *walk through every training example.* With 100 students, that's $i=1,2,\dots,100$.

The **inner sum**, $\sum_{k=1}^{K}$, means: *for this one student, walk through every class.* With three classes:

$$
\sum_{k=1}^{3}y_k\log(\hat y_k)=y_1\log(\hat y_1)+y_2\log(\hat y_2)+y_3\log(\hat y_3)
$$

## 🪄 The one-hot trick that quietly simplifies everything

Now here's the moment that makes the whole formula click. Suppose the true class is Class 2, so the one-hot target is:

$$
[y_1,y_2,y_3]=[0,1,0]
$$

and the model predicted:

$$
[\hat y_1,\hat y_2,\hat y_3]=[0.2,0.7,0.1]
$$

Plug into the loss:

$$
-\left[0\cdot\log(0.2)+1\cdot\log(0.7)+0\cdot\log(0.1)\right]
$$

Two of the three terms vanish, because they're multiplied by zero. What's left:

$$
\boxed{-\log(0.7)}
$$

which generalizes to:

$$
\boxed{-\log(\text{probability assigned to the correct class})}
$$

That's it. Underneath the intimidating double summation, categorical cross-entropy is only ever asking one question per student: *"How much probability did you give the class that was actually correct?"*

## 🔗 Same shape you've already learned

Set this next to what you already know:

$$
L_{\text{binary}}=-[y\log p+(1-y)\log(1-p)]
$$

$$
L_{\text{multi}}=-\sum_k y_k\log P_k
$$

Same idea, generalized. The one-hot vector is just doing, for $K$ classes, what the $(1-y)$ term did for 2.

## 😬 Why confident wrong answers are punished so much harder

Suppose the correct class is Class 2, and look at three possible model outputs:

- Predicts $P_2=0.9$ → loss $=-\log(0.9)\approx0.105$ — barely a scratch.
- Predicts $P_2=0.5$ → loss $=-\log(0.5)\approx0.693$ — noticeably worse.
- Predicts $P_2=0.01$ → loss $=-\log(0.01)\approx4.605$ — a steep penalty.

The model isn't just graded on *"did you get the class right."* It's graded on *how much conviction it put behind the right answer* — which is a far richer training signal than a simple yes/no ever could be.

## 🔁 The training loop, laid out plainly

```text
Input X
   ↓
Calculate class logits
   ↓
Apply Softmax
   ↓
Get probabilities
   ↓
Compare with true one-hot labels
   ↓
Calculate categorical cross-entropy
   ↓
Calculate gradients
   ↓
Update weights
   ↓
Repeat
```

Every update follows the same gradient descent rule you already know:

$$
\boxed{\theta_{\text{new}}=\theta_{\text{old}}-\alpha\frac{\partial L}{\partial\theta}}
$$

Loop it across many epochs, and the weights for every class get nudged, together, until the correct class starts winning more of the probability mass — for the students who actually belong to it.

## 🔮 Prediction: the easy half

Training was the hard part. Prediction, once the weights are learned, is short. For a new student $x=[7.0, 82]$:

$$
z=[2.5,\ 1.0,\ -0.5]\ \xrightarrow{\text{Softmax}}\ P=[0.844,\ 0.114,\ 0.042]
$$

$$
\arg\max(P)=1 \implies \boxed{\text{Class 1}}
$$

## 🧾 `predict()` vs. `predict_proba()` — now it actually makes sense

> *"Then what's the difference between `predict()` and `predict_proba()`?"*

`clf.predict(X)` gives you the final committed answer:

```text
[1]
```

`clf.predict_proba(X)` gives you the full distribution behind that answer:

```text
[[0.844, 0.114, 0.042]]
```

| Class   | Probability |
| ------- | ----------: |
| Class 0 |       0.844 |
| Class 1 |       0.114 |
| Class 2 |       0.042 |

$$
\boxed{\texttt{predict()} = \text{final decision}} \qquad \boxed{\texttt{predict\_proba()} = \text{probability distribution}}
$$

Two models can both predict "Class 1" and mean completely different things by it. One might output $[0.51, 0.48, 0.01]$ — barely committed, practically a coin flip against Class 0. The other might output $[0.98, 0.01, 0.01]$ — near total conviction. `predict()` erases that distinction entirely. `predict_proba()` is the only place it survives.

## 🌸 Grounding it: the Iris example

The classic Iris dataset carries this exact structure — three species, Setosa, Versicolor, and Virginica, encoded numerically as $0, 1, 2$, with two selected features (sepal length and petal length) making the whole thing visualizable:

```text
Sepal Length
       +
Petal Length
       ↓
Linear scores for 3 classes
       ↓
Softmax
       ↓
P(Setosa), P(Versicolor), P(Virginica)
       ↓
Highest probability
       ↓
Predicted species
```

Plot the feature space, and it splits into regions — each governed by whichever class currently holds the highest score:

```text
          Class 1
       /-------------\
      /               \
     /                 \
    /       Class 2     \
   /                     \
  /-----------+-----------\
              |
              |
           Class 3
```

The exact shape of those boundaries depends entirely on the learned weights — but the *logic* dividing the space is always the same argmax rule.

## 🧮 Sanity Check

Before moving on, make sure these three hold together in your head, because they're the entire chapter compressed:

1. **Logits** — one raw, unbounded score per class: $z_k=w_k^Tx+b_k$
2. **Softmax** — turns those scores into a distribution: $P_k=\dfrac{e^{z_k}}{\sum_j e^{z_j}}$, always summing to 1
3. **Categorical Cross-Entropy** — penalizes based on the probability given to the *correct* class only, everything else cancels via one-hot: $L=-\dfrac{1}{M}\sum_i\sum_k y_k^{(i)}\log P_k^{(i)}$

If you can rebuild those three lines from memory, you have the chapter.

## 🌉 ML Bridge

| Binary Logistic Regression | Softmax Regression         |
| --------------------------- | --------------------------- |
| One score $z$                | One score $z_k$ per class    |
| Sigmoid                      | Softmax                      |
| Two classes                  | $K$ classes                    |
| Binary Cross-Entropy         | Categorical Cross-Entropy    |
| One output probability       | $K$ output probabilities       |
| $p+(1-p)=1$                    | $\sum_k P_k=1$                  |

Nothing here is a new discipline bolted onto the old one. It's the same architecture — scores, a squashing function, a loss tied to those probabilities, gradient descent tightening the fit — stretched from two doors to $K$ doors.

## 🌀 Deepest Realization

Somewhere in the middle of learning Softmax, it's tempting to think the model is being asked, "did you pick the right class?" It isn't. Every training step, it's being asked something far more demanding: *"of everything you could have believed, how much of your belief did you put on the truth?"*

That's the real difference between a classifier that merely labels and one that reasons in probabilities — the labeling model can be right by accident; the probability model has to *commit*, and committing wrongly with high confidence costs it far more than committing wrongly with hesitation. Categorical cross-entropy doesn't grade the answer. It grades the conviction behind the answer.

## ✅ Final Takeaway

 *"Binary logistic regression asks: is this class 1 or class 0? Softmax Regression asks: given every possible class, how much belief should I place in each one — and it never lets you forget that belief has to add up to exactly one."*
