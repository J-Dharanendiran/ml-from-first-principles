# 🧾 The Ledger That Never Asks for the Answer: Gradient Boosting, Worked by Hand

> *"Training is when the models look at the answer key. Prediction is when the answer key is gone, and the corrections are all that is left."*

In the last chapter you saw the **algorithm**: start with the mean, compute residuals, fit a tree to them, add a shrunken correction, repeat. It was clean, but it was also a bit abstract. Now we are going to put actual numbers on a board, five students, three models, one learning rate, and follow every single number until the final prediction falls out.

By the end you should be able to answer three questions without hesitating:

1. What exactly does Model 2 learn?
2. How do three models turn into **one** prediction?
3. What happens when a **brand-new** student, with no known salary, walks in?

---

## 📋 The Data on the Board

Five students, two features, one target:

| IQ  | CGPA | Actual salary $y$ |
| --: | ---: | ----------------: |
|  90 |    8 |                 3 |
| 100 |    7 |                 4 |
| 110 |    6 |                 8 |
| 120 |    9 |                 6 |
|  80 |    5 |                 3 |

The target values are:

$$
3,\;4,\;8,\;6,\;3
$$

We will use a learning rate of $\eta = 0.1$.

---

## 🟰 Model 1: The Starting Guess

For Gradient Boosting regression, the first model is the **mean of the target**:

$$
\frac{3+4+8+6+3}{5} = \frac{24}{5} = 4.8
$$

$$
\boxed{M_1(x)=4.8}
$$

Every student gets the same prediction, whatever their IQ or CGPA:

| IQ  | CGPA | Actual | Pred 1 |
| --: | ---: | -----: | -----: |
|  90 |    8 |      3 |    4.8 |
| 100 |    7 |      4 |    4.8 |
| 110 |    6 |      8 |    4.8 |
| 120 |    9 |      6 |    4.8 |
|  80 |    5 |      3 |    4.8 |

---

## 🧮 Residual 1: How Wrong Was Model 1?

Now we ask the only question Gradient Boosting ever really asks:

> **"How wrong was the current model, for each student?"**

The residual is:

$$
\text{Residual}=y-\hat y
$$

So:

* Student 1: $3-4.8=-1.8$
* Student 2: $4-4.8=-0.8$
* Student 3: $8-4.8=+3.2$
* Student 4: $6-4.8=+1.2$
* Student 5: $3-4.8=-1.8$

| IQ  | CGPA | Actual | Pred 1 | Residual 1 |
| --: | ---: | -----: | -----: | ---------: |
|  90 |    8 |      3 |    4.8 |       -1.8 |
| 100 |    7 |      4 |    4.8 |       -0.8 |
| 110 |    6 |      8 |    4.8 |       +3.2 |
| 120 |    9 |      6 |    4.8 |       +1.2 |
|  80 |    5 |      3 |    4.8 |       -1.8 |

A negative residual means Model 1 **over**-predicted. A positive one means it **under**-predicted. Notice they sum to zero: $-1.8-0.8+3.2+1.2-1.8=0$, which is exactly what you expect when the starting prediction is the mean.

---

## 🎯 Model 2 Does Not Learn the Salary

Here is where something important happens.

Model 2 receives:

```text
Inputs : IQ, CGPA
Target : Residual 1
```

So it is effectively learning:

$$
X \rightarrow [-1.8,\,-0.8,\,3.2,\,1.2,\,-1.8]
$$

It is learning an answer to a different question:

> *"Given IQ and CGPA, how much should I correct Model 1?"*

Let's imagine the tree it builds. IQ looks useful for separating the residuals:

```text
              IQ > 105?
              /       \
            NO         YES
            ↓           ↓
       negative      positive
       residual      residual
```

Why would the tree pick that? Look at the data.

**IQ ≤ 105:**

```text
IQ 90  → residual -1.8
IQ 100 → residual -0.8
IQ 80  → residual -1.8
```

All negative.

**IQ > 105:**

```text
IQ 110 → residual +3.2
IQ 120 → residual +1.2
```

All positive.

So the tree can learn:

```text
Low IQ region  → negative correction
High IQ region → positive correction
```

That is how Model 2 starts to fix Model 1.

---

## 🤔 "Model 2 Gave Me 2.0. Is That the Predicted Salary?"

Suppose Model 2 outputs, for one particular student:

$$
M_2(x)=2.0
$$

You might ask:

> *"Does that mean the predicted salary is 2.0?"*

**No.**

It means:

> **"Add approximately 2.0 to the current prediction."**

And since the learning rate is $\eta=0.1$, the actual correction applied is:

$$
0.1\times 2.0 = 0.2
$$

$$
\text{New prediction} = 4.8 + 0.2 = \boxed{5.0}
$$

This is what the `Pred2` column on the board is heading toward:

$$
\boxed{\text{Pred2}=\text{Pred1}+\eta M_2}
$$

$$
\text{Pred2}=4.8+0.1\,M_2
$$

---

## 🔍 Residual 2: What Error Is Still Left?

After Model 2 has made its correction, we ask again:

> *"How much error remains?"*

If the actual salary was $8$ and the new prediction is $5$:

$$
\text{Residual 2}=8-5=\boxed{3}
$$

There is still a $+3$ error, so **Model 3** is trained on these new residuals, and the cycle continues.

---

## 👤 Following One Student Through All Three Models

Numbers click best when you ride along with a single person. Take this student:

```text
IQ = 110
CGPA = 6
Actual salary = 8
```

**Model 1.** Everyone gets the mean:

$$
\text{Pred1}=4.8
$$

The error is $8-4.8=3.2$, so this student needs a $+3.2$ correction.

**Model 2.** Suppose Model 2 learns from the features that students like this need a correction of $M_2=2.0$. With $\eta=0.1$:

$$
\text{Correction}_2 = 0.1(2.0)=0.2
$$

$$
\text{Pred2}=4.8+0.2=\boxed{5.0}
$$

Better than 4.8, but still wrong. The new residual:

$$
8-5=3
$$

**Model 3.** Trained on that new residual. Suppose it outputs $M_3=2.5$. Again, this is **not** a salary prediction, it is another correction:

$$
0.1(2.5)=0.25
$$

$$
\text{Pred3}=5.0+0.25=\boxed{5.25}
$$

The ledger for this student:

```text
Model 1:   4.80
Model 2:  +0.20
Model 3:  +0.25
-----------------
Final:     5.25
```

(These tree outputs, 2.0 and 2.5, are **hypothetical**, chosen to show the mechanics. A later section shows what Model 2 would actually output on this exact data.)

---

## 🤝 "So Where Did the Three Models Come Together?"

> *"Three models, one answer. Do they vote? Do they average? Where exactly do they combine?"*

Neither. They don't vote. They don't average. They don't each independently predict a salary. They **add corrections sequentially**:

$$
\text{Pred1}=M_1
$$

$$
\text{Pred2}=M_1+\eta M_2
$$

$$
\text{Pred3}=M_1+\eta M_2+\eta M_3
$$

So with $\eta = 0.1$:

$$
\boxed{\text{Pred3}=M_1+0.1M_2+0.1M_3}
$$

Now you can read each term for what it means:

$$
\underbrace{M_1}_{\text{starting prediction}} + \underbrace{0.1M_2}_{\text{correction 1}} + \underbrace{0.1M_3}_{\text{correction 2}}
$$

> Don't read it as *"three models predicted three salaries and we added them."*
> Read it as *"start with Model 1's prediction, let Model 2 make a small correction, let Model 3 make another small correction."*

It isn't random addition. It is **sequential error correction.**

## 🆕 "A New Student Walks In. How Do We Predict Them?"

Here is the question the whole example has been building to. After training, a new student arrives:

```text
IQ = 115
CGPA = 7
```

> *"We don't know their salary. So how do we predict it?"*

We send this student's features through **every already-trained tree**.

**Step 1: Model 1.** It is just the mean:

$$
M_1=4.8
$$

```text
New student → Model 1 → 4.8
```

**Step 2: Model 2.** Send IQ = 115, CGPA = 7 through the trained Model 2. Suppose it outputs $M_2=2.0$:

$$
0.1(2)=0.2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad 4.8+0.2=5.0
$$

**Step 3: Model 3.** Same features through trained Model 3. Suppose $M_3=2.5$:

$$
0.1(2.5)=0.25 \quad\Rightarrow\quad 5.0+0.25=5.25
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{Prediction}=5.25}
$$

---

## ⚖️ The Distinction That Matters Most Before You Code

> *"During training I calculated residuals at every step. Do I calculate them for the new student too?"*

No, and this is probably the single most important thing to understand before coding Gradient Boosting.

**During training**, we know the actual salary, so we can compute errors:

```text
Actual
  ↓
Prediction
  ↓
Residual
  ↓
train next tree
```

$$
\boxed{\text{prediction → residual → next tree}}
$$

**During prediction**, the actual salary is exactly what we're trying to find out. There is nothing to subtract from, so **no residual is calculated and nothing is retrained**. The new point simply flows through the finished trees:

```text
                 New student
                     │
                     ▼
                 Model 1
                     │
                    4.8
                     │
                     ▼
                 Model 2
                     │
                  + 0.20
                     │
                     ▼
                 Model 3
                     │
                  + 0.25
                     │
                     ▼
                  FINAL
                   5.25
```

$$
\boxed{\text{new point → every tree → add corrections → final prediction}}
$$

A trained ensemble is a chain of correction machines:

```text
New student
   │
   ▼
┌─────────┐
│ Model 1 │ → "Start at 4.8"
└────┬────┘
     ▼
┌─────────┐
│ Model 2 │ → "+0.20"
└────┬────┘
     ▼
┌─────────┐
│ Model 3 │ → "+0.25"
└────┬────┘
     ▼
    5.25
```

Summary of the two modes:

| Mode | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Training** | Each new model learns the remaining errors of the combined previous models |
| **Prediction** | The new point passes through every trained model; outputs are combined using the learning rate |

## 🔬 Supplement: What Model 2 Would *Actually* Output Here

The board used hypothetical tree outputs ($M_2=2.0$). Because the dataset is tiny, we can compute what a one-split tree on `IQ > 105` would really output. A regression tree's leaf predicts the **mean of the target values that land in it**, and here the target is Residual 1.

**Left leaf (IQ ≤ 105):** residuals $-1.8,\,-0.8,\,-1.8$

$$
\frac{-1.8-0.8-1.8}{3}=\frac{-4.4}{3}\approx -1.467
$$

**Right leaf (IQ > 105):** residuals $+3.2,\,+1.2$

$$
\frac{3.2+1.2}{2}=2.2
$$

With $\eta=0.1$, the corrections are $0.1\times(-1.467)\approx -0.147$ and $0.1\times 2.2=0.22$:

| IQ  | Actual | Pred 1 | $M_2$ (leaf mean) | Pred 2 | Residual 2 |
| --: | -----: | -----: | ----------------: | -----: | ---------: |
|  90 |      3 |    4.8 |            -1.467 |  4.653 |     -1.653 |
| 100 |      4 |    4.8 |            -1.467 |  4.653 |     -0.653 |
| 110 |      8 |    4.8 |            +2.2   |  5.02  |     +2.98  |
| 120 |      6 |    4.8 |            +2.2   |  5.02  |     +0.98  |
|  80 |      3 |    4.8 |            -1.467 |  4.653 |     -1.653 |

Two things to notice. First, **every residual got a little smaller in magnitude** (for example, $3.2 \to 2.98$ and $-1.8 \to -1.653$): that is one boosting step working. Second, the improvement is modest, because $\eta=0.1$ deliberately takes small steps. This is also why real models use many trees rather than three.

This supplement is my own arithmetic on the board's data, assuming a single split at `IQ > 105`. A real tree-building algorithm chooses its own split, so treat it as an illustration rather than the board's exact tree.

## ✅ Sanity Check

1. **Mean:** $(3+4+8+6+3)/5 = 24/5 = 4.8$ ✔
2. **Residuals sum to zero:** $-1.8-0.8+3.2+1.2-1.8=0$ ✔
3. **Split purity:** IQ ≤ 105 gives only negative residuals; IQ > 105 gives only positive ones ✔
4. **First correction:** $4.8+0.1\times2.0=5.0$ ✔
5. **Second correction:** $5.0+0.1\times2.5=5.25$ ✔
6. **Ledger:** $4.8+0.20+0.25=5.25$ ✔
7. **Residual after Model 2:** $8-5=3$ ✔
8. **Supplement:** $-4.4/3\approx-1.467$ and $4.4/2=2.2$ ✔

## 🌉 ML Bridge

This chapter's example is small enough to hold in your head, but nothing about the mechanism changes at scale.

* A real `GradientBoostingRegressor` runs the same loop for **100 trees by default**, not three.
* Its trees are not one-split stumps but, by default, depth-3 trees.
* Its first model is the same: the mean of the target (for squared-error loss).
* Its prediction is the same ledger: the initial value plus the learning-rate-scaled outputs of every tree.

That last point is why `predict()` on a trained model is fast and needs no labels: it never computes a residual. It just runs the features down each tree and adds up the corrections.

---

## 🌀 Deepest Realization

The residual is a tool of **training only**. It exists because, while learning, you have the answer key. Once training ends, the residuals have done their job and vanish. What remains is not a pile of predictions but a **fixed recipe of corrections**: "start at 4.8, then apply whatever each tree says this input deserves."

So the model never memorizes *answers*. It memorizes *how to adjust a guess*.

## ✅ Final Takeaway

> **In Gradient Boosting, a prediction is never three models voting. It is one starting guess plus a chain of small, learning-rate-scaled corrections. During training, residuals tell the next tree what to fix. During prediction, there are no residuals: a new point just flows through every trained tree, collecting corrections, and the sum is the answer.**
