# 🗳️ Why Three 70% Models Can Beat One 70% Model: The Math Behind Voting Ensembles

> *"A crowd doesn't have to be brilliant. It has to be competent and not identically wrong."* 🎯

The last chapter gave you the map: Voting, Stacking, Bagging, Boosting. Now we zoom into the very first stop, **Voting**, and ask the question that makes people suspicious of ensembles in the first place.

> 🤔 *"How can combining three models that are each only 70% accurate possibly produce something better than 70%?"*

That is not a philosophy question. It has an exact answer, and we are going to compute it.

---

## 🧩 The Problem: Why Trust Only One Model?

Suppose you train three classification models, each independently, on the data:

- Model 1
- Model 2
- Model 3

The basic ensemble idea is one sentence:

> **Instead of trusting one model, ask several models and combine their answers.**

A new data point arrives and goes to all three:

```text
                    New data point
                         │
           ┌─────────────┼─────────────┐
           ↓             ↓             ↓
        Model 1       Model 2       Model 3
           ↓             ↓             ↓
       Class A        Class A        Class B
              \          |          /
               \         |         /
                └── Majority Vote ──┘
                         ↓
                      Class A
```

That is **Voting**.

> 🤔 *"But why would asking three models help? Won't they just repeat each other?"*

Sometimes, yes. That is the whole story of this chapter. Let's begin with a case where they don't.

---

## 🐱 When One Mistake Gets Overruled

```text
Actual answer = Cat

Model 1 → Cat ✅
Model 2 → Cat ✅
Model 3 → Dog ❌
```

```text
Cat: 2 votes
Dog: 1 vote

Final prediction → Cat ✅
```

**One model's mistake was overridden by the other two.** That is the heart of ensemble learning.

---

## 🪞 The Catch: Three Models, One Mistake

Now imagine all three models make **exactly the same** mistake:

```text
Actual = Cat

Model 1 → Dog ❌
Model 2 → Dog ❌
Model 3 → Dog ❌

Voting → Dog ❌
```

The ensemble confidently repeats the error. Three people agreeing incorrectly in a conference room. 😄

What you want instead:

```text
Model 1 → Dog ❌
Model 2 → Cat ✅
Model 3 → Cat ✅
```

Now voting fixes it.

> 🤔 *"So what does it mean for models to be 'independent'? Do they need to be mathematically independent?"*

Not in the strict statistical sense. The practical version is:

> **Their errors should not be highly correlated.**

Compare:

```text
DIVERSE ERRORS                      IDENTICAL ERRORS
Model 1 mistakes: A, B, C, D        Model 1 mistakes: A, B, C, D
Model 2 mistakes: E, F, G, H        Model 2 mistakes: A, B, C, D
Model 3 mistakes: I, J, K, L        Model 3 mistakes: A, B, C, D
   → voting helps a lot                → voting helps very little
```

So "more models = better" is **not** the rule. The rule is closer to:

> **More sufficiently diverse models can provide a better aggregate decision.**

---

## 🏛️ The Democracy Analogy

The instructor explains diversity with democracy. You ask three people the same question.

**Case 1: everyone has the same background.** They share the same knowledge and blind spots, so they may all be wrong together.

```text
Person 1 → Wrong ❌
Person 2 → Wrong ❌
Person 3 → Wrong ❌
```

Majority voting doesn't save you.

**Case 2: different backgrounds.**

```text
Person 1 → Engineer 🛠️
Person 2 → Painter 🎨
Person 3 → Doctor 🩺
```

One person's weakness may be another's strength.

In ML, diversity can come from:

- different algorithms
- different subsets of data
- different features
- different training samples
- different model parameters
- different randomization

The transcript's main point is simply that the models shouldn't behave identically.

---

## ⚙️ How Voting Actually Combines Answers

### For classification: the mode

```text
Model 1 → Class A
Model 2 → Class A
Model 3 → Class B

Final → Class A
```

$$
\text{Prediction} = \operatorname{mode}(y_1, y_2, y_3)
$$

where `mode` means the most frequent prediction.

### For regression: the mean

Voting isn't limited to classification. Suppose three regression models predict:

```text
Model 1 → 75
Model 2 → 80
Model 3 → 77
```

$$
\hat y = \frac{75 + 80 + 77}{3} = \frac{232}{3} \approx 77.33
$$

| Problem type | Combination rule |
|---|---|
| **Classification** | Majority vote 🗳️ |
| **Regression** | Average of predictions ➗ |

### ✅ Sanity Check

75 + 80 = 155, plus 77 = **232**. 232 ÷ 3 = 77.333... ✔️

## 🔢 The Big Calculation: Three Models at 70%

Now the question we opened with. Set up the assumptions:

```text
Model 1 accuracy = 70%
Model 2 accuracy = 70%
Model 3 accuracy = 70%
```

and **assume their errors are independent**. For each model:

$$
P(C) = 0.7 \qquad P(W) = 0.3
$$

where **C = correct** and **W = wrong**.

### Step 1: List every possible outcome

Each of three models is either correct or wrong, so there are:

$$
2^3 = 8
$$

combinations. Because the models are independent, we multiply their probabilities:

| Model 1 | Model 2 | Model 3 | Probability |
|:---:|:---:|:---:|---:|
| C | C | C | 0.7³ = **0.343** |
| C | C | W | 0.7 × 0.7 × 0.3 = **0.147** |
| C | W | C | **0.147** |
| W | C | C | **0.147** |
| C | W | W | **0.063** |
| W | C | W | **0.063** |
| W | W | C | **0.063** |
| W | W | W | 0.3³ = **0.027** |

### ✅ Sanity Check: do they cover everything?

$$
0.343 + 0.147 + 0.147 + 0.147 + 0.063 + 0.063 + 0.063 + 0.027 = 1
$$

Probabilities of all outcomes must sum to 1, and they do. (Spot-check: 0.7 × 0.7 × 0.3 = 0.49 × 0.3 = 0.147, and 0.7 × 0.3 × 0.3 = 0.7 × 0.09 = 0.063.)

### Step 2: Which outcomes give a correct majority?

With three models, the ensemble is right whenever **at least two are correct**:

```text
CCC
CCW
CWC
WCC
```

### Step 3: Add them up

All three correct:

$$
P(CCC) = 0.343
$$

Exactly two correct (three arrangements, each 0.147):

$$
3 \times 0.147 = 0.441
$$

Total:

$$
0.343 + 0.441 = 0.784
$$

$$
\boxed{P(\text{ensemble correct}) = 78.4\%}
$$

```text
Individual model              → 70%
Three-model voting ensemble   → 78.4%   (under the independence assumption)
```

> 🤔 *"So where did the extra 8.4% come from?"*

From **cancelled mistakes**. Walk the eight outcomes:

| Outcome | Ensemble result |
|---|---|
| ✅ ✅ ✅ | Correct |
| ✅ ✅ ❌ | Correct (one mistake overruled) |
| ✅ ❌ ✅ | Correct (one mistake overruled) |
| ❌ ✅ ✅ | Correct (one mistake overruled) |
| ✅ ❌ ❌ | **Wrong** |
| ❌ ✅ ❌ | **Wrong** |
| ❌ ❌ ✅ | **Wrong** |
| ❌ ❌ ❌ | **Wrong** |

The ensemble essentially says:

> *"I don't care whether one model screws up. I care whether the **majority** screws up."*

---

## 🎲 The 50% Line: Why Each Model Must Beat a Coin Flip

> 🤔 *"Does this trick work for any models? What if my models are bad?"*

For a binary classification problem, random guessing gets about **50%**. The lecture argues each model should do better than that:

$$
P(\text{correct}) > 0.5
$$

Let's prove it instead of trusting it.

### The general formula

Let every model have accuracy $p$, independent of the others.

- All three correct: $p^3$
- Exactly two correct (three arrangements): $3p^2(1-p)$

$$
P(\text{ensemble correct}) = p^3 + 3p^2(1-p)
$$

Expand:

$$
= p^3 + 3p^2 - 3p^3 = 3p^2 - 2p^3
$$

### When is the ensemble better than a single model?

We need:

$$
3p^2 - 2p^3 > p
$$

Move everything to one side:

$$
3p^2 - 2p^3 - p > 0
$$

Factor:

$$
p(1-p)(2p-1) > 0
$$

For $0 < p < 1$, both $p$ and $(1-p)$ are positive, so the only factor that decides the sign is:

$$
2p - 1 > 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{p > 0.5}
$$

### ✅ Sanity Check on the factoring

Multiply it back: $p(1-p)(2p-1) = p(2p - 1 - 2p^2 + p) = p(3p - 2p^2 - 1) = 3p^2 - 2p^3 - p$. ✔️ That matches the line above it.

### Three test cases

| Individual accuracy $p$ | Calculation of $3p^2 - 2p^3$ | Ensemble accuracy | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| **0.7** | 3(0.49) − 2(0.343) = 1.47 − 0.686 | **0.784** | Better than 0.7 ✅ |
| **0.5** | 3(0.25) − 2(0.125) = 0.75 − 0.25 | **0.5** | No improvement, coin flips stay coin flips 🪙 |
| **0.3** | 3(0.09) − 2(0.027) = 0.27 − 0.054 | **0.216** | **Worse** than 0.3 ❌ |

That last row is the warning label. Weak models thrown together do **not** magically become intelligent. Below 50%, the majority vote amplifies the wrongness.

---

## 🕳️ The Hidden Assumption: Independence

Everything above multiplied probabilities like:

$$
P(C, C, W) = P(C_1)\,P(C_2)\,P(W_3) = 0.7 \times 0.7 \times 0.3
$$

That multiplication is only legal if the models are **independent**. Real models often aren't. They may:

- use the same training data
- learn similar patterns
- use similar features
- make similar mistakes

So **78.4% is not a guarantee.** It is what you'd get in the idealized case. Three real 70% models might land anywhere between 70% and 78.4%, depending on how correlated their errors are. That is exactly why the lecture keeps hammering on diversity.

---

## ⚖️ Two Ingredients, Both Required

```text
Ingredient 1 → Models reasonably accurate (above 50% in this binary argument)
Ingredient 2 → Models make DIFFERENT mistakes (low error overlap)
```

You want:

```text
Accuracy       → reasonably high
Error overlap  → reasonably low
```

Not:

```text
Accuracy       → high
Error overlap  → extremely high   ← three copies of one opinion in different hats 🎩
```

### A weaker model can still earn its seat

```text
Model A = 90% accurate
Model B = 88% accurate
Model C = 52% accurate
```

Model C looks terrible, but if it makes very *different* mistakes from A and B, it can still contribute useful information.

And the reverse:

```text
Model A = 95%
Model B = 95%
Model C = 95%
```

That does **not** automatically give an excellent ensemble if all three make the same errors.

$$
\boxed{\text{Individual quality matters, but diversity matters too.}}
$$

## 🗺️ The Complete Mental Model

```text
                ENSEMBLE LEARNING
                       │
                       ↓
              Train multiple models
                       │
             ┌─────────┼─────────┐
             ↓         ↓         ↓
          Model 1   Model 2   Model 3
             │         │         │
             ↓         ↓         ↓
        Prediction Prediction Prediction
             │         │         │
             └─────────┼─────────┘
                       ↓
                Combine outputs
                       │
             ┌─────────┴─────────┐
             ↓                   ↓
        Classification        Regression
             ↓                   ↓
       Majority voting       Average
```

$$
\text{Classification: Final prediction} = \text{majority vote}
$$

$$
\text{Regression: Final prediction} = \text{mean of predictions}
$$

$$
\text{Success} \approx \text{Good individual models} + \text{Diverse errors}
$$

### The lecture's argument, in eight steps

1. Train multiple models.
2. Let each make its own prediction.
3. Combine the predictions.
4. Classification → majority vote.
5. Regression → average.
6. Diversity matters because different models make different mistakes.
7. Under independence assumptions, combining models can produce a stronger result.
8. In the simple binary majority-vote case, each model needs to beat random guessing for the ensemble to outperform it.

## 🔗 ML Bridge: Where This Lands in Your Work

For your Stroke Prediction system (classification), the practical recipe is: train a few *different* algorithms on the same data, check that each beats a naive baseline, then check whether their errors overlap. If they make the same mistakes, a voting ensemble won't help. If they disagree in useful ways, it might.

---

## 🌀 Deepest Realization

> **Voting doesn't make any single model smarter. It makes the *group's mistake* harder to commit, because a wrong answer now needs the majority to be wrong at the same time.**

The whole proof compresses to one inequality: $p > 0.5$. And the whole caveat compresses to one word: **independence**.

---

## ✅ Final Takeaway

> *"An ensemble wins when its members are each better than a coin flip and wrong in different places. Three 70% models can reach 78.4% on paper, but only as far as their mistakes refuse to line up."* 🗳️
