# The Sigmoid Derivative: Why It Simplifies to Something Beautiful

> *"Most derivatives give you a completely different-looking expression. Sigmoid gives you back something that looks like itself. That's not coincidence — it's design."*

---

## What We're Finding

The sigmoid function is:

$$\sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}$$

It squashes any real number into the range (0, 1). The derivative tells us the *slope* of this curve at any point $z$. And what we're going to discover is that the derivative can be written entirely in terms of the sigmoid's own output.

$$\frac{d\sigma}{dz} = \sigma(z)(1 - \sigma(z))$$

That formula will emerge cleanly from the algebra. But before we derive it, let's see why finding it matters.

---

## Why This Matters for Logistic Regression

When you train a Logistic Regression model, you have:

$$z = w^T x + b$$

Then sigmoid converts that linear combination into a probability:

$$\hat{y} = \sigma(z)$$

When gradient descent tries to optimize this, it needs to know: *how much does the output change if I nudge $z$ a tiny bit?* That's exactly what the derivative answers. And because the derivative of sigmoid can be computed from the sigmoid's output itself (without needing to recalculate $e^{-z}$), gradient descent becomes efficient and elegant.

This is not a coincidence. Sigmoid was chosen, in part, *because* its derivative has this property.

---

## The Derivation: Step by Step

### Step 1: Rewrite with a negative exponent

Start with:

$$\sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}$$

Rewrite the denominator using exponent rules:

$$\sigma(z) = (1 + e^{-z})^{-1}$$

This looks like a power function, and power functions have a clean derivative rule: if $f(x) = [g(x)]^{-1}$, then $f'(x) = -[g(x)]^{-2} \cdot g'(x)$.

That's the **chain rule** in disguise.

---

### Step 2: Apply the chain rule

Using the rule $\frac{d}{dz}[u^{-1}] = -u^{-2} \frac{du}{dz}$, where $u = 1 + e^{-z}$:

$$\frac{d\sigma}{dz} = -(1 + e^{-z})^{-2} \cdot \frac{d}{dz}(1 + e^{-z})$$

Now we need to differentiate the inside: $1 + e^{-z}$.

---

### Step 3: Differentiate the inside

The derivative of the constant 1 is 0.

For $e^{-z}$, apply the chain rule again:

$$\frac{d}{dz} e^{-z} = e^{-z} \cdot \frac{d}{dz}(-z) = e^{-z} \cdot (-1) = -e^{-z}$$

So:

$$\frac{d}{dz}(1 + e^{-z}) = -e^{-z}$$

Substitute back:

$$\frac{d\sigma}{dz} = -(1 + e^{-z})^{-2} \cdot (-e^{-z})$$

The negatives cancel:

$$\frac{d\sigma}{dz} = \frac{e^{-z}}{(1 + e^{-z})^2}$$

This is correct, but it's not yet in the useful form.

---

### Step 4: The magical simplification

Here's where it gets elegant. We want to express this derivative using $\sigma(z)$ itself, not $e^{-z}$.

Recall:
$$\sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}$$

What is $1 - \sigma(z)$?

$$1 - \sigma(z) = 1 - \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}$$

Put over a common denominator:

$$1 - \sigma(z) = \frac{1 + e^{-z}}{1 + e^{-z}} - \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}} = \frac{e^{-z}}{1 + e^{-z}}$$

Now multiply them together:

$$\sigma(z) \cdot (1 - \sigma(z)) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}} \cdot \frac{e^{-z}}{1 + e^{-z}}$$

$$= \frac{e^{-z}}{(1 + e^{-z})^2}$$

But that's exactly what we derived for $\frac{d\sigma}{dz}$!

$$\boxed{\frac{d\sigma}{dz} = \sigma(z)(1 - \sigma(z))}$$

Done. No approximation. No tricks. Pure algebra, and the answer is clean.

---

## Why This Simplification Matters

Most derivatives don't simplify like this. If you differentiate a random function $f(x) = x^3 e^{2x}$, you get something that looks completely different from the original.

But sigmoid is special: **its derivative can be calculated directly from its output**.

If someone tells you $\sigma(z) = 0.8$, you don't need to remember $z$ or recalculate $e^{-z}$. You can immediately compute:

$$\frac{d\sigma}{dz} = 0.8 \cdot (1 - 0.8) = 0.8 \cdot 0.2 = 0.16$$

This efficiency matters in practice. When you're backpropagating gradients through millions of neural network nodes, recalculating exponentials is expensive. Being able to compute the derivative from the cached forward-pass activation (the sigmoid output) is a huge computational win.

---

## What the Derivative Tells Us: The Shape of Sigmoid

Let's check some specific values:

| $\sigma(z)$ | $\sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)(1-\sigma(z))$ | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| 0.5 | 0.5 × 0.5 = **0.25** | Maximum slope |
| 0.9 | 0.9 × 0.1 = 0.09 | Steep → flat |
| 0.99 | 0.99 × 0.01 = 0.0099 | Nearly flat |
| 0.01 | 0.01 × 0.99 = 0.0099 | Nearly flat |
| 0.1 | 0.1 × 0.9 = 0.09 | Steep → flat |

**Key observation:** The derivative is *largest* when $\sigma(z) = 0.5$ and *smallest* when the sigmoid output approaches either 0 or 1.

This makes visual sense. The sigmoid has an S-shaped curve:

- **At the middle** ($z \approx 0$): the curve is steep. Small changes in $z$ produce large changes in output.
- **At the extremes** ($z \to -\infty$ or $z \to +\infty$): the curve is nearly flat. Changes in $z$ barely move the output.

And derivative = slope, so naturally: steep curve → large derivative, flat curve → small derivative.

---

## A Visual Perspective

Imagine the sigmoid curve as a hill:

```
        ___
       /   \       At the peak (z ≈ 0), the hill is steep.
      /     \      Slope is maximum.
     /       \_
    /          \__   On the shoulders, the hill flattens out.
   /              \  Slope approaches zero.
```

The derivative $\sigma(z)(1 - \sigma(z))$ is telling you: *how steep is the hill right here?*

When the output is 0.5, you're near the middle of the S-curve — the steepest part — so the slope is largest.

When the output is 0.99, you're on the right shoulder where the curve has flattened, so the slope is tiny.

---

## Why This Matters in Neural Networks

This property creates an important dynamic in deep learning:

**Gradient vanishing problem:** If a node's sigmoid output is very close to 0 or 1 (the node is "saturated"), the gradient through that node becomes extremely small. The learning signal barely flows backward. This was a major headache for training deep neural networks with sigmoid, and it's one reason ReLU activation functions became popular — they don't saturate.

**Good region for learning:** The steepest gradient region (around $\sigma(z) = 0.5$) is where learning is most effective. This is why weight initialization strategies are designed to keep activations in the mid-range early in training.

The derivative $\sigma(z)(1 - \sigma(z))$ elegantly captures both of these insights at once.

## The Complete Derivation Chain

If you want to remember the whole thing in order:

$$\sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}$$

↓ *Rewrite with exponent*

$$= (1 + e^{-z})^{-1}$$

↓ *Chain rule*

$$\frac{d\sigma}{dz} = -(1 + e^{-z})^{-2} \cdot (-e^{-z})$$

↓ *Simplify*

$$= \frac{e^{-z}}{(1 + e^{-z})^2}$$

↓ *Recognize $\sigma(z)$ and $1 - \sigma(z)$ in this expression*

$$= \sigma(z)(1 - \sigma(z))$$

Each step flows from the last. Nothing is summoned from nowhere.

---

## Connection to Logistic Regression Training

When you minimize cross-entropy loss for Logistic Regression:

$$J = -\frac{1}{n}\sum_i [y_i \log \hat{y}_i + (1-y_i)\log(1-\hat{y}_i)]$$

and take the derivative with respect to the weights $w$, the chain rule connects:

$$\frac{\partial J}{\partial w} = \frac{\partial J}{\partial \hat{y}} \cdot \frac{\partial \hat{y}}{\partial z} \cdot \frac{\partial z}{\partial w}$$

That middle term — $\frac{\partial \hat{y}}{\partial z}$ — is exactly $\sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)(1 - \sigma(z))$.

And because this derivative is expressible using the sigmoid's own output (which was computed in the forward pass), the gradient computation in backpropagation is clean and efficient.

This is why sigmoid was such a natural choice for Logistic Regression: not just because it outputs probabilities, but because its derivative has this computational property.

---

## The Key Insight

Don't memorize the formula $\sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)(1 - \sigma(z))$ as a standalone fact.

Understand *why it simplifies*:

1. The derivative starts as $\frac{e^{-z}}{(1 + e^{-z})^2}$ — looks messy.
2. But you recognize that $\frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}} = \sigma(z)$ (the sigmoid itself).
3. And $\frac{e^{-z}}{1 + e^{-z}} = 1 - \sigma(z)$ (the complement).
4. So the messy expression becomes $\sigma(z) \times (1 - \sigma(z))$ — clean.

The elegance isn't luck. It's a consequence of the sigmoid's structure: it naturally decomposes into $\sigma(z)$ and $1 - \sigma(z)$ in every useful place.

That's the entire lesson.

---

## Final Takeaway

> *"The derivative of sigmoid simplifies to its own output times its complement because of how the sigmoid is constructed. This isn't a mathematical curiosity — it's the reason sigmoid became the activation function of choice for Logistic Regression, and it shapes how gradients flow through neural networks to this day."*
