# 🎯 The Curve That Moved With You

> *"Not to memorize the ROC curve first — but to understand the chain of problems that makes it necessary."*

That was the warning before anything else got explained. And it's the right place to start, because ROC has a way of getting handed to people as a finished picture — a curve, an area, a number — with none of the *reasoning* that built it. So before the curve, there's a quieter correction that has to be made first:

> **ROC is not primarily a method for directly selecting one "best threshold."**
> It shows how the classifier behaves across **all possible thresholds**, letting you compare the trade-off between True Positive Rate and False Positive Rate. The actual threshold chosen depends on the application and the relative cost of errors.

Hold that thought. Everything below exists to prove it.

---

## 1. First, what problem are we even solving?

Start with a binary classification problem — the classic one:

> Predict whether an email is **Spam** or **Not Spam**.

- Input features (X): words, sender info, links
- Target (y): `1` = Spam, `0` = Not Spam

A model learns $X \rightarrow y$. It feels like it should just output *Spam* or *Not Spam* directly. But most classifiers don't work that way internally. They output a **score or probability**:

| Email | Predicted spam probability |
|---|---:|
| A | 0.95 |
| B | 0.82 |
| C | 0.63 |
| D | 0.48 |
| E | 0.21 |

That number is the whole story. Everything else — thresholds, confusion matrices, ROC, AUC — is just what happens *after* the model hands you a probability instead of an answer.

---

## 2. A probability is not a decision

Logistic Regression, for instance, gives you $P(y=1|x) = 0.82$ — an 82% estimate for the positive class. But somewhere, that number has to collapse into `0` or `1`. That collapse needs a rule, and the rule is a **threshold**:

$$
P(y=1|x) \geq T \Rightarrow \hat{y} = 1 \qquad P(y=1|x) < T \Rightarrow \hat{y} = 0
$$

With $T = 0.5$:

```text
0.95 → 1
0.82 → 1
0.63 → 1
0.48 → 0
0.21 → 0
```

> **The model produces a continuous score. The threshold converts that score into a discrete class.**

That sentence is doing more work than it looks like. Because now a question opens up that most people never think to ask.

---

## 3. Why 0.5 at all?

Why not $0.3$? Why not $0.9$? There's nothing sacred about $0.5$ — it's just a common default. And the moment the threshold moves, the predictions move with it. Which means:

> **Your errors change.**

This is the exact seam ROC is stitched out of.

---

## 4. Watching it happen with real numbers

Ten emails, true labels known:

| Email | Actual | Probability |
|---|---:|---:|
| A | 1 | 0.95 |
| B | 1 | 0.85 |
| C | 1 | 0.75 |
| D | 1 | 0.60 |
| E | 1 | 0.40 |
| F | 0 | 0.90 |
| G | 0 | 0.70 |
| H | 0 | 0.50 |
| I | 0 | 0.30 |
| J | 0 | 0.10 |

Notice **F**: actually not spam, but the model hands it a 0.90. The model is confidently wrong about that one — and that single row is why a threshold can never be a purely mathematical decision. It's a trade you're choosing to make.

### At threshold 0.5

| Email | Actual | Prob | Prediction |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 1 | 0.95 | 1 |
| B | 1 | 0.85 | 1 |
| C | 1 | 0.75 | 1 |
| D | 1 | 0.60 | 1 |
| E | 1 | 0.40 | 0 |
| F | 0 | 0.90 | 1 |
| G | 0 | 0.70 | 1 |
| H | 0 | 0.50 | 1 |
| I | 0 | 0.30 | 0 |
| J | 0 | 0.10 | 0 |

Reading off the confusion counts: $TP = 4$ (A, B, C, D), $FN = 1$ (E), $FP = 3$ (F, G, H), $TN = 2$ (I, J).

$$
\begin{bmatrix} TP=4 & FN=1 \\ FP=3 & TN=2 \end{bmatrix}
$$

---

## 5. Are the two mistakes equally bad?

> *Not necessarily.* This is one of the most important ideas in the whole subject.

Look at the two ways this model can fail:

- **False Positive** — a real email gets flagged as spam. You might lose something you needed.
- **False Negative** — spam slips into your inbox. Annoying, but rarely catastrophic.

Different costs, same "wrongness" label. That asymmetry is exactly why nobody should accept $T = 0.5$ on faith and walk away.

---

## 6. Turning the strictness knob

Raise the threshold to $T = 0.75$:

| Email | Prob | Prediction |
|---|---:|---:|
| A | 0.95 | 1 |
| B | 0.85 | 1 |
| C | 0.75 | 1 |
| D | 0.60 | 0 |
| E | 0.40 | 0 |
| F | 0.90 | 1 |
| G | 0.70 | 0 |
| H | 0.50 | 0 |
| I | 0.30 | 0 |
| J | 0.10 | 0 |

Now $TP=3$, $FN=2$, $FP=1$, $TN=4$. False positives dropped from 3 → 1. But false negatives climbed from 1 → 2.

**That trade-off is the heart of ROC.**

Think of the threshold as a dial:

- **Low threshold** ("I'll call it positive even without much confidence") → more true positives caught, but more false alarms too. $TPR \uparrow$, $FPR \uparrow$.
- **High threshold** ("only when I'm very sure") → fewer false alarms, but more real positives missed. $TPR \downarrow$, $FPR \downarrow$.

There is no setting of that dial that improves both at once. That's not a limitation of this particular model — it's the shape of the trade-off itself.

---

## 7. Naming the two measurements

**True Positive Rate** (Recall, Sensitivity):

$$
TPR = \frac{TP}{TP + FN}
$$

$TP + FN$ is *every actual positive that exists*. So TPR answers:

> **Of all the actual positive cases, how many did my model successfully detect?**

**False Positive Rate**:

$$
FPR = \frac{FP}{FP + TN}
$$

$FP + TN$ is *every actual negative that exists*. So FPR answers:

> **Of all the actual negative cases, how many did my model incorrectly flag as positive?**

Plugging in threshold 0.5: $TPR = \frac{4}{5} = 0.8$, $FPR = \frac{3}{5} = 0.6$ → point $(0.6, 0.8)$.

Plugging in threshold 0.75: $TPR = \frac{3}{5} = 0.6$, $FPR = \frac{1}{5} = 0.2$ → point $(0.2, 0.6)$.

Two thresholds, two points, one visible trade-off: FPR fell from $0.6 \to 0.2$, but TPR fell too, $0.8 \to 0.6$. Exactly as predicted.

---

## 8. So what does ROC actually do?

Instead of asking "is 0.5 good?" or "is 0.75 good?" — ask a bigger question:

> **Let's evaluate the classifier at *every* threshold, and see the whole shape of the trade-off.**

Sweep $T$ across $1.0, 0.9, 0.8, \dots, 0.1, 0$. At each value, compute $(FPR, TPR)$. Plot every point. Connect them.

That plot is the **ROC Curve** — Receiver Operating Characteristic.

```text
Model
  ↓
Probability scores
  ↓
Choose threshold
  ↓
Predictions
  ↓
Confusion Matrix
  ↓
TPR and FPR
  ↓
Change threshold → repeat
  ↓
Plot all points
  ↓
ROC Curve
```

> **ROC is not some mysterious new prediction algorithm.** The model is already trained. It already produces scores. ROC just keeps changing the decision threshold and watches what happens.

---

## 9. The two ends of the curve

**Threshold = 1**: nobody clears the bar, everyone predicted `0`. $TP = 0 \Rightarrow TPR = 0$; $FP = 0 \Rightarrow FPR = 0$. The curve starts near $(0,0)$.

**Threshold = 0**: everyone clears the bar, everyone predicted `1`. Every actual positive gets caught, $TPR = 1$ — but every actual negative gets falsely flagged too, $FPR = 1$. The curve ends near $(1,1)$.

So the curve always runs from $(0,0)$ to $(1,1)$ — the two extremes of "refuse everything" and "accept everything."

---

## 10. What "good" looks like on this map

A strong classifier assigns high probabilities to real positives and low probabilities to real negatives, cleanly. That means somewhere in the middle, a threshold exists where $TPR \approx 1$ *while* $FPR \approx 0$ — the **top-left corner**, $(0,1)$.

- $FPR = 0$ → no false alarms
- $TPR = 1$ → every real positive caught

That's classification heaven — rare enough in real data that seeing it should make you suspicious before it makes you happy. 😄

A **random classifier**, with no real discriminative power, produces roughly $TPR = FPR$ — the diagonal from $(0,0)$ to $(1,1)$. A useful model's curve sits above that diagonal, bowing toward the top-left.

---

## 11. Then comes AUC — and it is *not* the same thing as ROC

- **ROC** — the curve itself: $FPR$ vs. $TPR$ across thresholds.
- **AUC** — *Area Under that Curve*. A single number summarizing it.

$AUC = 1.0$ → perfect discrimination. $AUC \approx 0.5$ → roughly random. Below $0.5$ generally means the model is doing worse than chance in its current orientation — though flipping the score's direction could recover useful signal from it.

---

## 12. Here's the correction that started all of this

It's tempting to write:

> "ROC tells us the correct threshold."

That's too simple, and it's worth stating plainly why. **ROC does not know the cost of a false positive versus a false negative in your specific application.** It shows you *what's possible* — every trade-off point that exists — not which one you should stand on.

- **Cancer screening**: missing a real case ($FN$) can be far more costly than a false alarm. You'd likely accept a higher $FPR$ to push $TPR$ up.
- **Spam filtering**: losing a legitimate email can matter more than one spam message slipping through. You'd likely favor a low $FPR$, even at the cost of $TPR$.
- **Fraud detection**: the right point depends on investigation cost, customer friction, and fraud loss — none of which live inside the ROC curve itself.

> There is no universal $T = 0.5$, and no universal $T = 0.7$. **The "best" threshold depends on the problem, not the curve.**

This also means the popular shortcut — "pick the point closest to $(0,1)$" — is just *one* rule among several, minimizing $\sqrt{(FPR-0)^2 + (TPR-1)^2}$ as if both errors deserved equal weight. If your application treats false positives as far worse than false negatives, the geometrically "closest" point may be the wrong one to choose on purpose.

---

## 13. Why not just maximize accuracy and skip all this?

Imagine 9,900 negatives and only 100 positives. A model that predicts `0` for everyone scores:

$$
Accuracy = \frac{9900}{10000} = 99\%
$$

Looks fantastic. But it caught **zero** of the 100 positives — $TPR = 0$. The model is useless for the one thing it was built to find, while wearing a 99% badge. This is exactly why ROC-based evaluation resists being fooled by imbalance in a way raw accuracy can't.

---

## 🩺 ML Bridge

Swap "spam email" for the running example this whole series has been anchored to: **stroke prediction**. The asymmetry gets sharper, not softer. A **false negative** — telling someone they're low-risk when they're actually high-risk — carries a very different weight than a **false positive** — flagging someone as high-risk who turns out fine. In that setting, you'd deliberately move the threshold *down*, accepting more false alarms, to push $TPR$ as close to $1$ as the model allows. ROC is what lets you *see* that trade-off before you commit to it — it just never tells you where to stop.

---

## 🌀 Deepest Realization

Forget the word "threshold" for a moment. Every example the model has ever seen gets a **ranking score** — a position in a sorted line, highest confidence to lowest:

```text
A = 0.95
B = 0.91
C = 0.88
D = 0.73
E = 0.61
F = 0.54
G = 0.32
H = 0.17
```

Moving the threshold from high to low is just walking down that line, labeling more and more of it "positive." ROC measures:

> **How many actual positives do I recover as I accept more false positives?**

That's the real subject. Not one decision — the model's ability to *rank* the two classes apart from each other, independent of any single cutoff. Two datasets with the same underlying separation:

```text
Positive: 0.95  0.90  0.85  0.80  0.75
Negative: 0.60  0.50  0.40  0.30  0.10
```

— clean, separable, a threshold between 0.60 and 0.75 splits them almost perfectly. Versus:

```text
Positive: 0.95  0.70  0.55  0.30  0.20
Negative: 0.90  0.75  0.60  0.40  0.10
```

— overlapping, no threshold does the job cleanly. The ROC curve is really asking one question underneath everything else:

> **How well can this model separate the two classes as I move the decision boundary through them?**

---

## ✅ Final Takeaway

> **A classification model gives scores. The threshold decides what counts as positive. Changing the threshold changes the balance between detecting positives and generating false alarms. And the ROC curve visualizes that trade-off across every threshold at once — a map of what's possible, not an answer to what's right.**
