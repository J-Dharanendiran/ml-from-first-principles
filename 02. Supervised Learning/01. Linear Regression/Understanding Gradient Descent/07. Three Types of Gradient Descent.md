# ⚙️ The Three Ways a Model Learns to Stop Being Wrong

> *"How much of the dataset should we use before updating the coefficients?"*

That one question — asked and answered — is the entire reason Gradient Descent comes in three flavors. Not three algorithms. One algorithm, wearing three different outfits, depending on how much data it's willing to look at before it moves.

You had 10,000 rows sitting in front of you.

```
Row 1
Row 2
Row 3
...
Row 10,000
```

And the model, at every single step, had to decide: *do I wait for all of them, or do I move now?*

---

## 🐢 Batch Gradient Descent — The Patient One

Batch Gradient Descent refuses to move an inch until it has seen **everything**.

```
10,000 Rows
     ↓
Predict all 10,000 rows
     ↓
Calculate all 10,000 errors
     ↓
Compute one gradient
     ↓
Update β
```

It doesn't flinch after Row 1. It doesn't flinch after Row 500. It sits, patiently, grading the *entire* class before writing a single note in the margin. Hence the name — it uses the whole **batch**.

Take a tiny dataset:

| CGPA | Salary |
|------|--------|
| 8 | 15 |
| 7 | 13 |
| 9 | 18 |
| 6 | 11 |

Batch GD predicts Row 1, Row 2, Row 3, Row 4 — then, only then — calculates **one** average gradient and updates `m` and `b`. One update. That's it.

**✅ Advantages:** stable, smooth, mathematically exact.
**❌ Disadvantage:** imagine 10 million rows. Ten million predictions, ten million errors, ten million calculations — before the model dares to take one tiny step.

---

## ⚡ Stochastic Gradient Descent — The Impatient One

Then patience runs out.

Instead of waiting for the whole batch, Stochastic Gradient Descent updates **immediately** — after every single row.

```
Take Row 1 → Predict → Compute error → Update β
Take Row 2 → Predict → Update β
Take Row 3 → Update β
```

*Stochastic* literally means **random** — the data is shuffled before each epoch so the model never memorizes an order. Four rows no longer mean one update. Four rows mean **four updates**.

**✅ Advantages:** blazing fast, handles enormous datasets, tiny memory footprint.
**❌ Disadvantage:** the gradient becomes noisy. Picture climbing a mountain in heavy fog — instead of walking smoothly downhill, you keep second-guessing your direction:

```
Ideal Path:        SGD's Path:
   ↓                   ↓
   ↓                   ↘
   ↓                   ↑
   ↓                   ↙
Minimum              →  Minimum
```

It still reaches the bottom. It just zig-zags the whole way there.

---

## ⚖️ Mini-Batch Gradient Descent — The Compromise

And, as usual, humans looked at two extremes and did what humans always do — split the difference.

Not the entire dataset. Not one row. **Small groups.**

```
10,000 rows, batch size 100

Rows 1–100    → Update
Rows 101–200  → Update
Rows 201–300  → Update
```

Picture a class of 300 students taking an exam:

- **Batch GD** — the teacher waits for all 300 papers, then updates their understanding once.
- **SGD** — the teacher checks one paper at a time, updating after each.
- **Mini-Batch GD** — the teacher checks 20 papers, updates, checks the next 20, updates again. Balanced.

| Property | Batch GD | Stochastic GD | Mini-Batch GD |
|---|---|---|---|
| Data per update | Entire dataset | 1 sample | Small batch (32, 64, 128...) |
| Updates per epoch | 1 | Number of rows | Number of batches |
| Speed | Slow | Fast | Moderate |
| Memory | High | Very low | Moderate |
| Gradient quality | Exact | Noisy | Good approximation |
| Convergence | Smooth | Zig-zag | Smooth enough |
| Common usage | Small datasets | Online learning | Modern ML & Deep Learning |

Frameworks like TensorFlow and PyTorch train almost everything on mini-batches, because they buy back the speed of SGD without paying its full noise tax.

**One sentence to remember each by:**
- Batch GD: use *all* samples, then update once.
- Stochastic GD: use *one* sample, then update immediately.
- Mini-Batch GD: use a *small group*, then update once.

## 🤯 The Question That Cracks It Open

You said something that almost every beginner eventually stumbles into, but rarely says out loud:

> *"So gradient descent actually does y − ŷ for every row?? But how? I haven't heard this concept till now!!"*

Until that moment, the story in your head was probably: *"Gradient Descent updates m and b."*

But that's the **last** step. Before anything can be updated, the model has to first ask a much humbler question:

> **"How wrong am I — on every single training example?"**

Let's walk through one full epoch, on three rows only:

| CGPA | Salary (y) |
|---|---|
| 7 | 10 |
| 8 | 12 |
| 9 | 15 |

Starting guesses: `m = 1`, `b = 0`. Bad guesses, on purpose.

**Step 1 — Predict every row.**

$$\hat{y} = mx + b$$

| CGPA | Actual y | Prediction ŷ |
|---|---|---|
| 7 | 10 | 7 |
| 8 | 12 | 8 |
| 9 | 15 | 9 |

This is what `y_pred = model.predict(X)` — or, by hand, `y_pred = m * X + b` — is quietly doing underneath. Feed it 3 rows, get 3 predictions. Feed it 10,000 rows, get 10,000 predictions.

**Step 2 — Compute every error.**

$$\text{Error} = y - \hat{y}$$

| Actual | Predicted | Error |
|---|---|---|
| 10 | 7 | 3 |
| 12 | 8 | 4 |
| 15 | 9 | 6 |

These are the **residuals** — the model's full confession of how wrong it currently is, row by row.

**Step 3 — Use all of those errors together.**

The gradient doesn't fire off after any single error. It waits, collects `3, 4, 6`, and folds them into the loss and the gradient at once. Look at the intercept's derivative:

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial \beta_0} = -\frac{2}{N}\sum (y - \hat{y})$$

That summation symbol is doing something very literal — it means *Error(Row 1) + Error(Row 2) + Error(Row 3)*, which here is `3 + 4 + 6`. Every row gets a vote.

**Step 4 — Compute the gradient.**

$$-\frac{2}{3}(3 + 4 + 6) \quad \text{for } \beta_0$$

$$-\frac{2}{3}\big(3(7) + 4(8) + 6(9)\big) \quad \text{for the slope}$$

Each row whispers its own opinion about which way the line should move — and Gradient Descent averages all those opinions into a single direction.

---

### 🔎 Where This Was Hiding All Along

Go back to the loss function itself:

$$L = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2$$

That small subscript `i` was never decorative. It says: *for i = 1, compute y₁ − ŷ₁. For i = 2, compute y₂ − ŷ₂. For i = 3, compute y₃ − ŷ₃... all the way to N.* The summation was quietly instructing you to walk through every training example the entire time. It was always there — you just hadn't yet been forced to think about it operationally.

### 💻 In Code, It's Almost Suspiciously Simple

```python
X = np.array([7, 8, 9])
y = np.array([10, 12, 15])
m, b = 1, 0

y_pred = m * X + b     # [7, 8, 9]
error = y - y_pred     # [3, 4, 6]
```

$$
X = \begin{bmatrix} 7 \\ 8 \\ 9 \end{bmatrix}
\quad
\hat{y} = \begin{bmatrix} 7 \\ 8 \\ 9 \end{bmatrix}
\quad
y - \hat{y} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix}
$$

No explicit Python loop. NumPy applies the operation element-wise across the whole array — the "loop" is just hiding inside the math.

## ✅ Sanity Check

Think of the model as a teacher grading an exam for 300 students. The teacher doesn't glance at one paper and immediately rewrite the textbook. Instead:

1. Grade every paper.
2. See where students went wrong.
3. Summarize the mistakes.
4. Improve the textbook.

Gradient Descent does exactly this — predict every row, measure every error, average the information via derivatives, *then* update.

## 🌉 ML Bridge

This loop — **predict → measure error → compute gradients → update parameters → repeat** — is not specific to Batch Gradient Descent. It's the training loop underneath *every* variant. Stochastic GD runs it on one row at a time. Mini-Batch GD runs it on small groups. The mathematics inside the loop never changes; only how much data is fed into it before the update fires.

## 🌀 Deepest Realization

> The gradient is not computed from the dataset directly. It is computed from the **errors** produced *after* the model has made predictions on every training sample. Without that `y − ŷ` step, Gradient Descent has no way of knowing whether its current parameters are good or bad — and nothing to optimize at all.

This is the detail most textbooks compress into a single Σ symbol, leaving your intuition to reconstruct the rest on its own.

---

## ✅ Final Takeaway

> *"Every coefficient in a model only moves after the model has confessed, row by row, exactly how wrong it currently is."*
