# 📐 The Corner in the Loss: Deriving Lasso's Three Equations From Scratch

> *"First explain until the equation part, don't skip the steps and deriving the actual equation — like said in the transcript!"*

You've already met Ridge in the Stroke Prediction pipeline — the penalty that shrinks a coefficient toward zero but, no matter how hard you push it, never quite lets it arrive there. Lasso does something Ridge structurally cannot: it zeroes a coefficient out completely, dropping a feature like `bmi` or `avg_glucose_level` from the model as if it were never there. Before you can understand *why* Lasso gets stuck exactly at zero, you have to watch the algebra that produces the three separate equations governing its behavior. No skipping steps this time.

## Starting from the model you already trust

Strip the stroke dataset down to one feature — say `age` — predicting `stroke_risk_score`. The model is the plainest thing there is:

$$\hat{y}_i = mx_i + b$$

and ordinary least squares wants to minimize:

$$L = \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - mx_i - b)^2$$

Two unknowns sit in that expression: $m$ and $b$. Before touching the penalty, you need $b$ pinned down in terms of $m$ — and rather than pulling $b = \bar{y} - m\bar{x}$ out of thin air the way most explanations do, you differentiate for it.

**Differentiate with respect to $b$:**

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial b} = -2\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - mx_i - b)$$

Set it to zero, divide by $-2$:

$$\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - mx_i - b) = 0$$

Expand the sum — remembering that $b$ is a constant, so $\sum b = nb$:

$$\sum y_i - m\sum x_i - nb = 0$$

Isolate $b$:

$$nb = \sum y_i - m\sum x_i \quad\Rightarrow\quad b = \frac{\sum y_i}{n} - m\frac{\sum x_i}{n}$$

Recognize those fractions as means, and you land on the identity you'll use constantly:

$$b = \bar{y} - m\bar{x}$$

## Folding the intercept back into the model

> *That's just an equation for* $b$*. What does it actually do to the model?*

Substitute it back into $\hat{y}_i = mx_i + b$:

$$\hat{y}_i = mx_i + \bar{y} - m\bar{x} = \bar{y} + m(x_i - \bar{x})$$

Now look at the residual $y_i - \hat{y}_i$ with this new form substituted in:

$$y_i - \hat{y}_i = y_i - \big[\bar{y} + m(x_i - \bar{x})\big] = (y_i - \bar{y}) - m(x_i - \bar{x})$$

This single substitution is the reason every Lasso equation you'll ever see is written in terms of $(y_i - \bar{y})$ and $(x_i - \bar{x})$ instead of raw $y_i$ and $x_i$. It isn't stylistic — it falls directly out of eliminating $b$.

## Building the Lasso loss

Lasso adds an L1 penalty — the absolute value of the slope — onto the squared-error loss:

$$L = \sum_{i=1}^n \big[(y_i - \bar{y}) - m(x_i - \bar{x})\big]^2 + \lambda|m|$$

For algebraic convenience — purely to make the derivative land cleanly, not because Lasso needs double the punishment — this derivation scales the penalty to $2\lambda|m|$:

$$L(m) = \sum_{i=1}^n \big[(y_i - \bar{y}) - m(x_i - \bar{x})\big]^2 + 2\lambda|m|$$

And here the algebra runs into a wall. $|m|$ has a sharp corner at $m = 0$ — it isn't differentiable there. So the derivation has to split into three regimes: $m > 0$, $m < 0$, and the corner itself, $m = 0$.

## Case A: when $m > 0$

Here $|m| = m$, so the loss is:

$$L = \sum \big[(y_i - \bar{y}) - m(x_i - \bar{x})\big]^2 + 2\lambda m$$

**Differentiate the squared term.** Let $u = (y_i - \bar{y}) - m(x_i - \bar{x})$, so $\frac{du}{dm} = -(x_i - \bar{x})$. By the chain rule, $\frac{d}{dm}u^2 = 2u\frac{du}{dm}$, giving:

$$\frac{d}{dm}\big[(y_i-\bar{y})-m(x_i-\bar{x})\big]^2 = -2\big[(y_i-\bar{y})-m(x_i-\bar{x})\big](x_i-\bar{x})$$

Summed across all rows:

$$-2\sum \big[(y_i-\bar{y})-m(x_i-\bar{x})\big](x_i-\bar{x})$$

**Differentiate the penalty.** Since $\lambda$ is constant, $\frac{d}{dm}(2\lambda m) = 2\lambda$.

**Combine and set to zero:**

$$-2\sum\big[(y_i-\bar{y})-m(x_i-\bar{x})\big](x_i-\bar{x}) + 2\lambda = 0$$

Divide by 2:

$$-\sum(y_i-\bar{y})(x_i-\bar{x}) + m\sum(x_i-\bar{x})^2 + \lambda = 0$$

*(expanding the bracket: $(y_i-\bar{y})(x_i-\bar{x}) - m(x_i-\bar{x})^2$, then distributing the leading negative — this is the step most explanations rush past)*

Isolate $m$:

$$m\sum(x_i-\bar{x})^2 = \sum(y_i-\bar{y})(x_i-\bar{x}) - \lambda$$

$$\boxed{m = \frac{\sum(y_i-\bar{y})(x_i-\bar{x}) - \lambda}{\sum(x_i-\bar{x})^2}} \quad (m > 0)$$

## Case B: when $m < 0$

Now $|m| = -m$, so the penalty term becomes $2\lambda(-m) = -2\lambda m$, and its derivative flips sign to $-2\lambda$. Running the identical steps:

$$-2\sum\big[(y_i-\bar{y})-m(x_i-\bar{x})\big](x_i-\bar{x}) - 2\lambda = 0$$

$$m\sum(x_i-\bar{x})^2 = \sum(y_i-\bar{y})(x_i-\bar{x}) + \lambda$$

$$\boxed{m = \frac{\sum(y_i-\bar{y})(x_i-\bar{x}) + \lambda}{\sum(x_i-\bar{x})^2}} \quad (m < 0)$$

## Case: $m = 0$

At the corner itself, $|m| = 0$, and the ordinary — unregularized — slope expression sits underneath, unmodified by $\lambda$ in either direction:

$$m = \frac{\sum(y_i-\bar{y})(x_i-\bar{x})}{\sum(x_i-\bar{x})^2}$$

This isn't found by differentiating $|m|$ normally — you can't, right at the corner. It's a separate case, handled properly later with the subgradient. For now it's simply the third branch.

## The pattern, side by side

Naming $S = \sum(y_i-\bar{y})(x_i-\bar{x})$ and $D = \sum(x_i-\bar{x})^2$:

$$m>0:\ m=\frac{S-\lambda}{D} \qquad\qquad m<0:\ m=\frac{S+\lambda}{D}$$

```
                     Lasso
                       |
              -------------------
              |                 |
            m > 0             m < 0
              |                 |
           S − λ              S + λ
              |                 |
              ------- m=0 -------
```

## Why does subtraction happen on one side and addition on the other?

> *So the sign just... flips because of where m sits?*

Follow the derivative of the penalty alone, forgetting the rest of the loss for a moment:

$$|m| = \begin{cases} m & m>0 \\ -m & m<0 \end{cases} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \lambda|m| = \begin{cases}\lambda m & m>0\\-\lambda m & m<0\end{cases} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{d}{dm}\lambda|m| = \begin{cases}+\lambda & m>0\\-\lambda & m<0\end{cases}$$

That constant $\pm\lambda$ is the *entire* mechanism. On the positive side, $+\lambda$ gets moved to the other side of the equation and becomes a subtraction — $Dm = S - \lambda$. On the negative side, $-\lambda$ becomes an addition — $Dm = S + \lambda$. Nothing was designed by hand to say "if positive, subtract; if negative, add" — it falls straight out of how $|m|$ is defined on the two sides of the corner.

**Watch it numerically**, using round stand-ins for `age`'s $S$ and $D$ in the stroke model: $S = 100$, $D = 50$.

Positive side, increasing $\lambda$:

$$\lambda=0 \Rightarrow m=\frac{100}{50}=2 \qquad \lambda=50 \Rightarrow m=\frac{100-50}{50}=1 \qquad \lambda=100 \Rightarrow m=\frac{100-100}{50}=0$$

Negative side, with $S=-100$:

$$\lambda=0 \Rightarrow m=-2 \qquad \lambda=50 \Rightarrow m=-1 \qquad \lambda=100 \Rightarrow m=0$$

$$+2 \to +1 \to 0 \quad\text{(subtract)} \qquad\qquad -2 \to -1 \to 0 \quad\text{(add)}$$

Both directions are the same operation seen from opposite sides of zero: shrink the magnitude.

## "So any coefficient bigger than λ becomes zero"?

> *I understood this part. So any coefficient that exceeds the value of the lambda would set to zero! How? Coz it's us who decides the value of the penalty lambda...*

Close, but the comparison isn't quite `coefficient vs. λ` — it's `S vs. λ`, where $S$ is the raw, unregularized, data-driven signal (how strongly `age` correlates with `stroke_risk_score` before any penalty touches it).

$$S=100,\ \lambda=50 \Rightarrow m=\frac{100-50}{D}\ \text{(still positive)}$$
$$S=100,\ \lambda=100 \Rightarrow m=\frac{100-100}{D}=0\ \text{(exactly cancelled)}$$
$$S=100,\ \lambda=150 \Rightarrow m=\frac{100-150}{D}=\text{negative — but the } m>0 \text{ formula can't produce that}$$

That contradiction is the tell. The $m>0$ branch was *derived under the assumption* $m>0$; the moment it tries to output a negative number, the branch has invalidated its own premise. The optimizer can't just hop to the $m<0$ formula either — jumping from $0$ straight to $S+\lambda = 5$ would mean the penalty *increasing* the coefficient's magnitude, which contradicts the entire point of regularizing. So the coefficient halts at the only value that doesn't contradict anything: exactly zero.

$\lambda$ is the one thing you choose — a hyperparameter, tuned by cross-validation, not learned by the same gradient step that fits $m$. $S$ is not something you choose; it's what the data hands you. Lasso's real question, asked separately for every feature, is:

> *"Is this feature's contribution to reducing error worth paying the λ toll?"*

If `bmi`'s $S$ is weak and $\lambda$ is high enough to exceed it, `bmi` exits the model entirely. If `age`'s $S$ is strong, it can survive a much larger $\lambda$ before it's forced to zero.

## ✅ Sanity Check

- $b = \bar{y} - m\bar{x}$ comes from differentiating the loss with respect to $b$ — not asserted.
- Substituting $b$ back in is *why* every Lasso term is centered: $(y_i-\bar{y})$, $(x_i-\bar{x})$.
- $|m|$'s non-differentiability at zero is *why* the derivation needs three separate cases, not one.
- $m>0 \Rightarrow \frac{S-\lambda}{D}$ and $m<0 \Rightarrow \frac{S+\lambda}{D}$ are mirror images, both produced by the same $\pm\lambda$ that falls out of differentiating $|m|$.
- The comparison that decides whether a coefficient dies is $|S|$ against $\lambda$ — not the finished coefficient against $\lambda$.

## 🔗 ML Bridge

This is the exact reason Lasso performs *automatic feature selection* inside a pipeline like Stroke Prediction: crank $\lambda$ up during cross-validation, and features whose $S$ can't clear the bar — weak, redundant, or noisy predictors — get dropped to exactly zero, leaving a sparser, more interpretable model behind. Ridge, with $\lambda$ sitting in the denominator instead of contesting the numerator, can never do this — it can only make a coefficient small, never gone.

## 🌀 Deepest Realization

> Lasso never "decides" to zero a feature out the way a rule-based system would. The zero is a *forced consequence* of algebra refusing to contradict itself — the $m>0$ branch cannot output a negative number without violating its own assumption, and the $m<0$ branch cannot jump upward without violating the regularizer's entire purpose. Zero is the only point left standing between two branches that both refuse to cross it.

## Final Takeaway

> *"The corner in $|m|$ isn't a flaw in the math — it's the mechanism. Everything Lasso does, from centering the data around $\bar{x}$ and $\bar{y}$ to selecting features away entirely, is downstream of that one non-differentiable point at $m=0$."*
