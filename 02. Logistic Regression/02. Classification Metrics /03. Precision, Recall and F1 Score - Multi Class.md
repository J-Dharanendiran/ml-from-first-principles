# 🗳️ The Vote Each Class Gets: Multiclass Precision, Recall, and F1

> "When there are only two classes, the world is simple: right or wrong, Positive or Negative. But real classifiers rarely get that luxury. What happens the moment a third class walks into the room?"

You already earned the hard part. Binary Precision and Recall aren't mysteries to you anymore — you've sat with TP, FP, FN, and TN long enough that they feel like old coworkers. So when **Dog**, **Cat**, and **Rabbit** show up together in the same confusion matrix, the temptation is to assume the rules just broke.

They didn't. They multiplied.

---

## 1. The matrix that started it

Picture a model that's just finished predicting on 108 animals. Here's what it produced:

| Actual \ Predicted | Dog | Cat | Rabbit | Actual Total |
|---|--:|--:|--:|--:|
| **Dog** | 25 | 15 | 10 | 50 |
| **Cat** | 0 | 30 | 4 | 34 |
| **Rabbit** | 4 | 0 | 20 | 24 |
| **Predicted Total** | 29 | 45 | 34 | 108 |

$$
\text{Confusion Matrix} =
\begin{bmatrix}
25 & 15 & 10 \\
0 & 30 & 4 \\
4 & 0 & 20
\end{bmatrix}
$$

> "But this table has three rows and three columns — where did TP and FP even go?"

That question is the whole chapter. Before answering it, sit with what the table is actually telling you, because rows and columns are *not* interchangeable here.

**Rows are the truth.** The Dog row — `25, 15, 10` — means: of the animals that were *actually* dogs, 25 were correctly called Dog, 15 were mistaken for Cat, and 10 for Rabbit. Add them up and you get the real population of dogs in this dataset:

$$
25 + 15 + 10 = 50 \text{ actual Dogs}
$$

**Columns are the model's opinion.** The Dog column — `25, 0, 4` — means: of everything the model *labeled* Dog, 25 were right, 0 mislabeled cats, and 4 mislabeled rabbits. Add those and you get how trigger-happy the model was about the word "Dog":

$$
25 + 0 + 4 = 29 \text{ predicted Dogs}
$$

Fifty actual dogs. Twenty-nine predicted dogs. Already you can feel that something's off — the model is *under-calling* Dog. Keep that feeling. It'll be confirmed with a number soon.

---

## 2. The trick that makes three classes feel like two

> "So how do we calculate precision for Dog when Negative used to mean one specific thing, and now it has to mean two different animals at once?"

Here's the move, and it's disarmingly simple:

> **Pick one class. Call it Positive. Melt everything else into a single Negative.**

That's it. That's the entire "multiclass" trick — there's no new formula waiting behind a curtain. It's called a **one-vs-rest** interpretation, and it means you get to keep using the binary tools you already trust.

So for Dog:

$$
\boxed{Dog = Positive} \qquad \boxed{Cat + Rabbit = Negative}
$$

Reshape the original matrix around that split:

| Actual / Predicted | Predicted Dog | Predicted Not-Dog |
|---|--:|--:|
| **Actual Dog** | 25 | 15 + 10 |
| **Actual Not-Dog** | 0 + 4 | 30 + 0 |

Read straight off it:

$$
TP_{Dog} = 25 \qquad FP_{Dog} = 0+4=4 \qquad FN_{Dog} = 15+10=25
$$

The 3×3 monster just folded down into the exact binary shape you already know how to hold.

---

## 3. Dog gets judged

**Precision** asks: *of everything I called Dog, how much did I get right?*

$$
Precision_{Dog} = \frac{TP}{TP+FP} = \frac{25}{25+4} = \frac{25}{29} \approx 0.86
$$

$$
\boxed{Precision_{Dog} \approx 86\%}
$$

**Recall** asks a completely different question: *of everything that really was a Dog, how much did I find?*

$$
Recall_{Dog} = \frac{TP}{TP+FN} = \frac{25}{25+25} = \frac{25}{50} = 0.50
$$

$$
\boxed{Recall_{Dog} = 50\%}
$$

> "86% precision but only 50% recall — how can the same model be that confident and that blind about the same animal?"

Because those two numbers are answering different questions to different audiences. High precision, low recall reads like this:

> *"When I call something a Dog, I'm usually right — but I'm only finding half the dogs that exist."*

That single sentence carries more diagnostic weight than "the model got 80% accuracy" ever could. Accuracy hides exactly this kind of asymmetry. Precision and Recall refuse to.

---

## 4. Cat and Rabbit get the same treatment

The procedure doesn't change — only the diagonal cell and the row/column you're reading from does.

**Cat** — column `15, 30, 0`, row `0, 30, 4`:

$$
Precision_{Cat} = \frac{30}{15+30+0} = \frac{30}{45} \approx 0.67
$$
$$
Recall_{Cat} = \frac{30}{0+30+4} = \frac{30}{34} \approx 0.88
$$

**Rabbit** — column `10, 4, 20`, row `4, 0, 20`:

$$
Precision_{Rabbit} = \frac{20}{10+4+20} = \frac{20}{34} \approx 0.59
$$

And here's where the story needs to pause, because the original tutoring source made a mistake worth preserving rather than quietly fixing.

### 🔍 The correction

The source computed Rabbit's recall as:

$$
Recall_{Rabbit} \overset{?}{=} \frac{20}{34} \approx 0.58
$$

But 34 is the **predicted** Rabbit total — the column sum. Recall's own definition, established two sections ago, says the denominator has to be the **actual** positive total — the row sum. Rabbit's actual row is `4, 0, 20`:

$$
4 + 0 + 20 = 24 \text{ actual Rabbits}
$$

So the number consistent with the rest of the chapter — the one that actually answers "of the real rabbits, how many did the model find?" — is:

$$
\boxed{Recall_{Rabbit} = \frac{20}{24} \approx 83.3\%}
$$

Not 58%. That 58% figure was quietly reusing Rabbit's *precision* denominator for its *recall* calculation — an easy transcription slip, and exactly the kind that survives in teaching material because the arithmetic still "looks clean." It doesn't survive here.

---

## 5. Sanity Check ✅

| Class | Precision | Recall |
|---|--:|--:|
| **Dog** | 25/29 ≈ 86% | 25/50 = 50% |
| **Cat** | 30/45 ≈ 67% | 30/34 ≈ 88% |
| **Rabbit** | 20/34 ≈ 59% | 20/24 ≈ 83% *(corrected)* |

Notice the shape of it: Dog is precise but blind, Cat and Rabbit are both fairly generous finders (high recall) but noisier when they commit (lower precision). Three classes, three completely different personalities — and none of that texture survives if you collapse it into one accuracy number.

---

## 6. From three numbers to one: Macro Average

> "Three precisions, three recalls — but what if I need a single number for the whole model?"

**Macro averaging** is the plain arithmetic mean, nothing fancier:

$$
Macro\ Precision = \frac{Precision_{Dog}+Precision_{Cat}+Precision_{Rabbit}}{3} = \frac{0.86+0.67+0.59}{3} \approx 0.706
$$

$$
\boxed{Macro\ Precision \approx 70.6\%}
$$

The name "macro" is a clue about its politics: **every class gets exactly one vote**, no matter how many members it has. Dog casts one vote. Cat casts one vote. Rabbit — with only 24 examples in the whole dataset — casts a vote worth exactly the same as Dog's 50. If your dataset secretly had 10,000 dogs, 100 cats, and 20 rabbits, macro averaging wouldn't notice or care. It's built for situations where a rare class matters *just as much* as a common one — fraud, rare diseases, anything where "small but critical" describes the minority class.

---

## 7. When equal votes aren't fair: Weighted Average

Now imagine the opposite instinct: you *want* the big classes to matter more, because they represent more of reality. That's **weighted averaging** — give each class's score a vote proportional to how often it actually occurs:

$$
\boxed{Weight_i = \frac{Support_i}{Total\ Samples}}
$$

where **support** is just the row total — how many real examples of that class exist. For our animals:

$$
N = 108, \quad Support_{Dog}=50,\ Support_{Cat}=34,\ Support_{Rabbit}=24
$$

$$
Weighted\ Precision = 0.86\left(\frac{50}{108}\right) + 0.67\left(\frac{34}{108}\right) + 0.59\left(\frac{24}{108}\right)
$$

Dog, being nearly half the dataset, pulls the weighted average toward its own score far harder than Rabbit — which is exactly the point. Weighted averaging tells you *"how did the model do, given the actual mix of animals it had to deal with,"* rather than *"how did the model do if every class mattered equally."*

| Method | Class importance |
|---|---|
| **Macro** | Equal — every class, one vote |
| **Weighted** | Proportional — bigger class, bigger say |

Reach for macro when your classes are roughly balanced or the rare ones are the ones you actually care about. Reach for weighted when the dataset is genuinely imbalanced and you want the metric to reflect that imbalance honestly rather than pretend it away.

---

## 8. F1 shows up to referee Precision and Recall — again

Nothing new here either. Compute F1 per class, exactly like binary F1, then average.

$$
F1_i = 2\cdot\frac{Precision_i \times Recall_i}{Precision_i + Recall_i}
$$

**Dog:**
$$
F1_{Dog} = 2\cdot\frac{0.86 \times 0.50}{0.86+0.50} \approx 0.632 \;\Rightarrow\; \boxed{63.2\%}
$$

**Cat:**
$$
F1_{Cat} = 2\cdot\frac{0.667 \times 0.882}{0.667+0.882} \approx 0.759 \;\Rightarrow\; \boxed{75.9\%}
$$

**Rabbit** *(using the corrected recall, 0.833):*
$$
F1_{Rabbit} = 2\cdot\frac{0.588 \times 0.833}{0.588+0.833} \approx 0.690 \;\Rightarrow\; \boxed{69.0\%}
$$

And the single-number summary, **Macro F1**:

$$
Macro\ F1 = \frac{0.632+0.759+0.690}{3} \approx 0.694
$$

$$
\boxed{Macro\ F1 \approx 69.4\%}
$$

Dog's harsh 63.2% is the F1 score telling you the truth that "86% precision" alone was hiding — a model that misses half its dogs doesn't get to call itself a Dog-detector, no matter how confident it sounds when it does commit to the label.

---

## 9. The shortcut that replaces the whole chapter

Once you've done this by hand a few times, you don't need to rebuild the one-vs-rest table every time. The confusion matrix already contains the shortcut, sitting in plain sight:

$$
\boxed{Precision_i = \frac{Diagonal_i}{Column\ Total_i}} \qquad \boxed{Recall_i = \frac{Diagonal_i}{Row\ Total_i}}
$$

**Precision looks down a column** — "of what I predicted, how much was right?" **Recall looks across a row** — "of what was real, how much did I catch?"

$$
\boxed{P \rightarrow \text{Predicted (column)}} \qquad \boxed{R \rightarrow \text{Real / Actual (row)}}
$$

That one-line rule is the fastest way to read *any* N×N confusion matrix for the rest of your life.

---

## 🌉 ML Bridge

This is the exact machinery sitting underneath `sklearn.metrics.classification_report()`. Every time that function prints precision, recall, and f1-score for each class and then hands you a `macro avg` and a `weighted avg` row at the bottom, it did nothing more mysterious than what you just did by hand: one-vs-rest per class, diagonal-over-column, diagonal-over-row, then two different philosophies of averaging. When your Stroke Prediction model eventually gets extended past binary — or when you evaluate any classifier with more than two outcomes — this table *is* the report you'll be reading.

---

## 🌀 Deepest Realization

Accuracy is a single number that quietly averages away exactly the information you need to trust or distrust a model. A model can be 80% "accurate" while being a confident liar about Dogs and a generous guesser about Rabbits — two completely different failure modes, hiding under one clean-looking percentage. Precision and Recall don't average that away. And macro versus weighted isn't a technical footnote — it's you deciding, explicitly, whether a rare class deserves the same voice as a common one. That decision was always yours to make. Accuracy just made it for you, silently, without asking.

---

## ✅ Final Takeaway

> *"A confusion matrix doesn't need a new formula for every new class — it needs you to ask the same two questions, one class at a time: of what I predicted, how much was true? Of what was true, how much did I predict? Everything else — macro, weighted, F1 — is just deciding how loudly each class's answer gets to speak."*
