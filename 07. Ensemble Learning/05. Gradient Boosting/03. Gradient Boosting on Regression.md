# 🎚️ One Mean, Many Corrections: How Gradient Boosting Gives Every Input Its Own Answer

> *"The mean starts the prediction. The trees make it individual."*

Here is a puzzle that trips up almost everyone the first time they see Gradient Boosting for regression.

The first model predicts the **mean**, the same number for every row. The final model gives **different predictions for different inputs**. So somewhere between "everyone gets 60" and "this person gets 65, that person gets 53.8", something personal must enter the story. But the only ingredient we ever added was a pile of trees.

So you might be asking yourself:

> *"If the mean is the same for everybody, where do the individual predictions actually come from? And when a new test value arrives, do I recompute the mean?"*

This chapter answers both questions by following **one tiny dataset** from the first guess to the final prediction. Instead of five students and two features, we use the simplest setup that still shows everything: **one feature, four rows.**

---

## 🎯 What Problem Are We Solving?

We have training data that maps an input to a target:

$$
X \rightarrow y
$$

| Experience (years) | Salary |
| -----------------: | -----: |
|                  1 |     30 |
|                  2 |     40 |
|                  5 |     70 |
|                  8 |    100 |

We want to learn a function:

$$
\hat y = F(X)
$$

that predicts a **continuous value**. For example:

```text
Experience = 6
        ↓
Gradient Boosting model
        ↓
Predicted salary = ?
```

---

## 🧱 The Basic Idea

Gradient Boosting does **not** build one giant decision tree. Instead:

$$
\boxed{\text{Many small trees are built sequentially}}
$$

and each new tree tries to **correct the mistakes made by the current ensemble**. The final model is additive:

$$
\boxed{
F_M(x)=F_0(x)+\eta f_1(x)+\eta f_2(x)+\cdots+\eta f_M(x)
}
$$

where:

* $F_0(x)$ is the initial prediction
* $f_1, f_2, \ldots, f_M$ are successive weak learners, commonly shallow decision trees
* $\eta$ is the learning rate
* $M$ is the number of boosting rounds

The sentence to hold onto:

> **Each tree contributes a correction to what the previous trees already predicted.**

---

## 🟰 Step 1: Start With the Mean

For squared-error regression, Gradient Boosting begins with the mean target value. Our salaries are $30, 40, 70, 100$:

$$
F_0=\frac{30+40+70+100}{4}=\frac{240}{4}=60
$$

| Experience | Actual | Initial prediction |
| ---------: | -----: | -----------------: |
|          1 |     30 |                 60 |
|          2 |     40 |                 60 |
|          5 |     70 |                 60 |
|          8 |    100 |                 60 |

$$
\boxed{F_0(x)=60}
$$

for **every training row**. Hold this thought, because it is the source of the puzzle we opened with.

---

## 🧮 Step 2: Measure the Errors

For squared-error regression the residual is:

$$
r_i = y_i - \hat y_i
$$

| Experience | Actual | Prediction | Residual |
| ---------: | -----: | ---------: | -------: |
|          1 |     30 |         60 |      -30 |
|          2 |     40 |         60 |      -20 |
|          5 |     70 |         60 |      +10 |
|          8 |    100 |         60 |      +40 |

Read them as:

* $-30$: the prediction is **30 too high**
* $-20$: the prediction is **20 too high**
* $+10$: the prediction is **10 too low**
* $+40$: the prediction is **40 too low**

Now Gradient Boosting makes its one demand:

> *"I need a model that predicts these errors."*

---

## 🌳 Step 3: Train Tree 1 on the Residuals

This is the step that causes the most confusion, so read it slowly.

We are **not** training Tree 1 to predict salary. We train it to predict the **residuals**:

```text
Target for Tree 1:  [-30, -20, +10, +40]
```

Suppose Tree 1 finds a simple split:

```text
Experience < 3
       /       \
     YES       NO
      ↓         ↓
   residual   residual
    ≈ -25      ≈ +25
```

> *"Why -25 and +25? Where did those numbers come from?"*

They are **averages of the residuals that land in each leaf**. The left leaf holds the rows with residuals $-30$ and $-20$:

$$
\frac{-30+(-20)}{2}=-25
$$

The right leaf holds $+10$ and $+40$:

$$
\frac{10+40}{2}=25
$$

So Tree 1 outputs:

| Experience | Tree 1 correction |
| ---------: | ----------------: |
|          1 |               -25 |
|          2 |               -25 |
|          5 |               +25 |
|          8 |               +25 |

---

## 🐢 Step 4: Apply the Learning Rate

Take $\eta = 0.1$. We do **not** add Tree 1's whole correction. We add only 10% of it:

$$
F_1(x)=F_0(x)+0.1\,f_1(x)
$$

For Experience = 1:

$$
60+0.1(-25)=57.5
$$

For Experience = 8:

$$
60+0.1(25)=62.5
$$

Now the predictions have **started to differ**:

| Experience | Actual | Old prediction | Tree 1 | New prediction |
| ---------: | -----: | -------------: | -----: | -------------: |
|          1 |     30 |             60 |    -25 |           57.5 |
|          2 |     40 |             60 |    -25 |           57.5 |
|          5 |     70 |             60 |    +25 |           62.5 |
|          8 |    100 |             60 |    +25 |           62.5 |

They are still pretty bad, and that is intentional. One small tree is not supposed to solve everything.

---

## 🔁 Step 5: Recalculate the Residuals

Now compute what is left:

$$
r_i^{(2)} = y_i - F_1(x_i)
$$

* Experience 1: $30-57.5=-27.5$
* Experience 2: $40-57.5=-17.5$
* Experience 5: $70-62.5=+7.5$
* Experience 8: $100-62.5=+37.5$

| Experience | Actual | Current prediction | New residual |
| ---------: | -----: | -----------------: | -----------: |
|          1 |     30 |               57.5 |        -27.5 |
|          2 |     40 |               57.5 |        -17.5 |
|          5 |     70 |               62.5 |         +7.5 |
|          8 |    100 |               62.5 |        +37.5 |

$$
\boxed{\text{Tree 2 learns the mistakes that remain after Tree 1}}
$$

---

## 🌳 Step 6: Train Tree 2

Tree 2 receives:

```text
X = Experience
y = [-27.5, -17.5, +7.5, +37.5]
```

It finds its own splits and predicts its own corrections. Suppose it makes the same kind of split as before. The leaf averages are:

$$
\frac{-27.5+(-17.5)}{2}=-22.5 \qquad \frac{7.5+37.5}{2}=22.5
$$

| Experience | Tree 2 correction |
| ---------: | ----------------: |
|          1 |             -22.5 |
|          2 |             -22.5 |
|          5 |             +22.5 |
|          8 |             +22.5 |

Then:

$$
F_2(x)=F_1(x)+0.1\,f_2(x)
$$

For Experience = 1:

$$
57.5+0.1(-22.5)=55.25
$$

For Experience = 8:

$$
62.5+0.1(22.5)=64.75
$$

Again, the predictions move **a little closer** to the actual values.

---

## ♻️ And Then It Repeats

```text
                 Start
                   ↓
              Mean prediction
                   ↓
              Calculate errors
                   ↓
              Train Tree 1
                   ↓
          Add small correction
                   ↓
        Calculate remaining errors
                   ↓
              Train Tree 2
                   ↓
          Add small correction
                   ↓
        Calculate remaining errors
                   ↓
              Train Tree 3
                   ↓
                  ...
                   ↓
              Final model
```

In symbols:

$$
F_1(x)=F_0(x)+\eta f_1(x)
$$

$$
F_2(x)=F_1(x)+\eta f_2(x)
$$

$$
F_3(x)=F_2(x)+\eta f_3(x)
$$

and eventually:

$$
\boxed{
F_M(x)=F_0(x)+\eta\sum_{m=1}^{M}f_m(x)
}
$$

---

## 🆕 The Test-Time Question: Do We Recompute the Mean?

Training is finished. A new input arrives:

$$
X_{\text{new}}=6
$$

> *"Do I calculate a new mean for this point?"*

**No.** The mean $60$ was a value learned **once, during training**. It is the fixed starting point. Now the test point is sent through **every trained tree**, and each tree contributes a correction.

```text
Initial prediction
      ↓
     60
      +
Tree 1 correction  →  +25
      +
Tree 2 correction  →  +18
      +
Tree 3 correction  →   +9
      +
Tree 4 correction  →   -2
```

With $\eta=0.1$:

$$
F(6)=60+0.1(25)+0.1(18)+0.1(9)+0.1(-2)
$$

$$
=60+2.5+1.8+0.9-0.2=65
$$

$$
\boxed{65}
$$

The numbers here are **illustrative**; the mechanism is what matters. No new mean, no residuals, no retraining. Just: start at 60, then collect corrections.

---

## 🧭 Why Do Different Inputs Get Different Answers?

This is the answer to the puzzle we opened with.

Each tree sends different inputs down **different paths**, so each input collects a **different set of corrections**.

**Test point A: $X=1$**

Tree outputs $-25, -22, -15$:

$$
60+0.1(-25-22-15)=60+0.1(-62)=53.8
$$

**Test point B: $X=6$**

Tree outputs $+25, +18, +9$:

$$
60+0.1(25+18+9)=60+0.1(52)=65.2
$$

**Test point C: $X=9$**

It would land in yet another combination of leaves and collect yet another set of corrections.

So although

$$
F_0(x)=60 \quad \text{for everyone,}
$$

the later terms

$$
f_1(x),\;f_2(x),\;f_3(x),\;\ldots
$$

all **depend on the input $x$**. That is where the individuality comes from. It never came from the mean. It came from the trees.

---

## 🍃 What Does a Tree Actually Output?

A regression tree divides the feature space into regions:

```text
             Experience < 3?
               /         \
             Yes          No
             /             \
           -25             +25
```

Each leaf contains some training observations. For squared-error regression, the leaf prediction is typically the **mean target value of the observations in that leaf**. Here the "target" is the residual, which is why the leaves held averages of residuals.

So each tree makes **piecewise-constant predictions**, and Gradient Boosting adds those pieces together:

$$
\boxed{\text{Final prediction = starting value + many small corrections}}
$$

---

## 🧭 Where Does the Word "Gradient" Come From?

Everything above used the phrase *"calculate residuals."* That is a simplification that happens to be exact for squared error. Here is why.

Use the squared-error loss with the common ½ convention:

$$
L(y,F(x))=\frac{1}{2}\,(y-F(x))^2
$$

Differentiate with respect to the **prediction**:

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial F(x)}=F(x)-y
$$

So the **negative gradient** is:

$$
-\frac{\partial L}{\partial F(x)}=y-F(x)
$$

which is exactly the residual:

$$
\boxed{\text{negative gradient}=\text{residual}}
$$

That is why trees fitting residuals is, for this loss, the same as trees fitting the negative gradient. (Without the ½, the negative gradient is $2(y-F(x))$, proportional to the residual instead of equal to it. The tree learns the same direction either way.)

---

## 🌲 Why Not Just Use One Big Tree?

A single deep tree can become very complex and overfit the training data. Gradient Boosting takes a different route:

> Start simple. Make a small correction. Make another small correction. Keep improving.

```text
Tree 1  → learns the broad pattern
Tree 2  → fixes remaining errors
Tree 3  → fixes more errors
Tree 4  → fixes more errors
...
```

The individual trees are weak. **The ensemble is strong.** Complexity is accumulated gradually instead of built all at once.

## 🔬 Supplement: The Full Ledger After Two Trees

The lesson's Tree 2 numbers happen to be exactly what a one-split tree on `Experience < 3` produces for this data, so we can continue the ledger honestly. The next residuals are:

$$
30-55.25=-25.25 \qquad 40-55.25=-15.25
$$

$$
70-64.75=+5.25 \qquad 100-64.75=+35.25
$$

Here is how each row's error shrinks across rounds:

| Experience | Start (mean) | After Tree 1 | After Tree 2 |
| ---------: | -----------: | -----------: | -----------: |
|          1 |          -30 |        -27.5 |       -25.25 |
|          2 |          -20 |        -17.5 |       -15.25 |
|          5 |          +10 |         +7.5 |        +5.25 |
|          8 |          +40 |        +37.5 |       +35.25 |

Every error moves toward zero, but slowly. That slowness is the learning rate doing its job.

One more observation, which is my own and not from the lesson: a split at `Experience < 3` can never separate Experience 1 from Experience 2, so the gap between their residuals ($-30$ versus $-20$, a difference of 10) never closes by this tree. To fix that, a later tree must choose a **different split** (for example, between 1 and 2). This is one reason boosting uses many trees whose splits can change from round to round.

## 🗺️ The Complete Mental Model

```text
TRAINING
─────────────────────────────────────────
Training data
     │
     ▼
Calculate mean(y)  →  Initial prediction F₀
     │
     ▼
Calculate errors / negative gradients
     │
     ▼
Train Tree 1 on those errors  →  Add η × Tree 1
     │
     ▼
New predictions  →  New errors
     │
     ▼
Train Tree 2  →  Add η × Tree 2
     │
     ▼
...
     ▼
Final boosted model


PREDICTION
─────────────────────────────────────────
New X
 │
 ├──► Tree 1 ──► correction
 ├──► Tree 2 ──► correction
 ├──► Tree 3 ──► correction
 ├──► Tree 4 ──► correction
 └──► ...
          │
          ▼
Initial mean + all corrections
          │
          ▼
       Final ŷ
```

### The five things to remember

$$
\boxed{1.\ \text{Start with mean}(y)}
$$

$$
\boxed{2.\ \text{Find the errors}}
$$

$$
\boxed{3.\ \text{Train a small tree to predict those errors}}
$$

$$
\boxed{4.\ \text{Multiply its prediction by the learning rate and add it}}
$$

$$
\boxed{5.\ \text{Repeat}}
$$

And at prediction time:

$$
\boxed{
\text{prediction}=\text{initial mean}+\text{correction from Tree 1}+\text{correction from Tree 2}+\cdots
}
$$

## 🌉 ML Bridge

In scikit-learn's `GradientBoostingRegressor`, the same ideas map directly onto parameters you will actually set:

| Concept in this chapter | Parameter |
|---|---|
| $\eta$, the learning rate | `learning_rate` (default 0.1) |
| $M$, the number of boosting rounds | `n_estimators` (default 100) |
| How big each weak tree is | `max_depth` (default 3) |
| The starting value $F_0$ | the default initial estimator, which predicts the target average for squared-error loss |

So the "four rows, one split, two trees" toy model is the same machine as the library model, just with fewer, smaller parts.

---

## 🌀 Deepest Realization

The puzzle was never a puzzle. The model is not computing a mean for each row. It computes a mean **once**, as a shared baseline, and then learns **input-dependent corrections**. Individuality is not stored in the starting value; it is stored in the **routing** of each input through the trees.

> **The model is not "calculating the mean for each row." It calculates the mean once as the initial model, then learns input-dependent corrections through successive trees.**

## ✅ Final Takeaway

> **Gradient Boosting calculates the mean once, as a shared starting point, then builds trees that each learn what the ensemble still gets wrong. At prediction time there is no new mean and no residual: the new input travels through every tree, collects its input-specific corrections, and the learning-rate-scaled sum on top of the starting value is the answer. The mean starts the prediction. The trees make it individual.**
