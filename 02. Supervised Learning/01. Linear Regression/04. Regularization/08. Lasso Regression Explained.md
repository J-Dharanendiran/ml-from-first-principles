# 📐 The Penalty With a Corner: Why Lasso Can Delete a Feature

> "What if I don't just want small coefficients? What if I want some coefficients to become completely *unnecessary*?"

That question is the entire reason Lasso exists. You already know Ridge. You already know its whole philosophy: *fit the data, but don't let the coefficients get unnecessarily large.* Ridge is polite about it — a constant, gentle pressure that squeezes every coefficient a little smaller, but never quite closes the door on any of them.

Lasso is not polite. Lasso is willing to slam the door shut.

## 🧠 Forget Lasso for a moment — start with the fit

Ordinary linear regression, for multiple features, looks like this:

$$
\hat{y} = w_0 + w_1x_1 + w_2x_2 + \dots + w_px_p
$$

and it chooses coefficients purely to minimize error:

$$
J(w) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2
$$

It only cares about one thing: *fit the training data as well as possible.* Left unsupervised, that can produce unnecessarily large coefficients — especially with correlated features or noisy data. That's the gap Ridge walked into.

## 🌀 Ridge's answer, in one glance

Ridge adds an **L2 penalty** — the squared coefficients:

$$
J(w) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2 + \lambda\sum_{j=1}^{p}w_j^2
$$

Take $w_1 = 5, w_2 = -3, w_3 = 0.5$. Ridge penalizes them as:

$$
5^2 + (-3)^2 + (0.5)^2 = 25 + 9 + 0.25 = 34.25
$$

Ridge pushes coefficients continuously toward zero — but it almost never lands *on* zero. It turns $10 \to 6.4$, $3 \to 1.8$, $0.4 \to 0.15$. Small, non-zero, forever shrinking. That's called **shrinkage**, and it's where Ridge's story ends.

## ✂️ Lasso asks a sharper question

Instead of squaring the coefficients, Lasso uses their **absolute value**:

$$
J(w) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2 + \lambda\sum_{j=1}^{p}|w_j|
$$

This is the **L1 penalty**. Scikit-learn's actual formulation scales it slightly differently:

$$
\frac{1}{2n}\|y - Xw\|_2^2 + \alpha\|w\|_1
$$

but the spirit is identical. And the name gives away the ambition: **Lasso** stands for *Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator.* Two words matter here —

- **Shrinkage** — it pulls coefficients toward zero, same as Ridge.
- **Selection** — it can make coefficients *exactly* zero. Ridge can't promise that.

Suppose training gives you $w_1=8, w_2=3, w_3=0.2, w_4=-0.04, w_5=5$. Lasso might return:

$$
w_1=6.5,\ w_2=1.8,\ w_3=0,\ w_4=0,\ w_5=4.2
$$

Now $x_3$ and $x_4$ have vanished from the prediction entirely. They weren't shrunk. They were *removed.*

> Why does Lasso produce zero — really?

It's tempting to answer "because L1 penalizes more." That's incomplete, and honestly, it's the kind of answer that sounds smart while explaining nothing. The real reason is shape.

## 📊 The table that hides the whole story

| w | w² (Ridge) | \|w\| (Lasso) |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 2 | 4 | 2 |
| 1 | 1 | 1 |
| 0.5 | 0.25 | 0.5 |
| 0.1 | 0.01 | 0.1 |
| 0.01 | 0.0001 | 0.01 |

Ridge's $w^2$ is smooth everywhere. Lasso's $|w|$ has a sharp corner sitting right at zero. *That tiny-looking difference is the entire reason Lasso behaves differently.*

> Explain me that "tiny-looking difference is the entire reason Lasso behaves differently" part.

Fair. That sentence is the kind of line a textbook drops and then moves on, as if the reader is supposed to just nod. Let's actually open it up.

## ⚙️ The mechanism: what the derivative is doing

Training updates weights using the *derivative* of the penalty — this is the actual force pulling each coefficient around.

**Ridge's derivative:**

$$
\frac{d}{dw}(\lambda w^2) = 2\lambda w
$$

**Lasso's derivative:**

$$
\frac{d}{dw}(\lambda|w|) = \lambda \cdot \text{sign}(w), \qquad \text{sign}(w) = \begin{cases} +1 & w>0 \\ -1 & w<0 \end{cases}
$$

At $w=0$ the Lasso derivative doesn't exist in the ordinary sense — that's the corner. Instead we use a **subgradient**:

$$
\partial|w| = [-1, 1]
$$

That interval is the whole trick. It means that *at exactly zero*, the penalty can balance the pull from the data across an entire range of forces — not just one exact value. That's what lets $w=0$ become a genuine optimum instead of a coincidence.

## 🔢 Watch the numbers diverge

Take $w = 0.01$ and $\lambda = 1$.

| Method | Penalty gradient |
|---|---|
| Ridge | $2 \times 1 \times 0.01 = 0.02$ |
| Lasso | $1$ |

Lasso is pushing **fifty times harder**, on a weight that's already almost nothing. Extend the whole table:

| w | Ridge slope $2w$ | Lasso slope |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 2 | 4 | 1 |
| 1 | 2 | 1 |
| 0.5 | 1 | 1 |
| 0.1 | 0.2 | 1 |
| 0.01 | 0.02 | 1 |

**Ridge:** $4 \to 2 \to 1 \to 0.2 \to 0.02$ — the closer to zero, the gentler the pull.
**Lasso:** $1 \to 1 \to 1 \to 1 \to 1$ — the pull barely changes at all, all the way down.

Ridge behaves like a **rubber band**: the closer you get to the center, the weaker it pulls.
Lasso behaves like a **magnet**: it pulls with nearly the same strength right up until the weight snaps onto zero.

## 🌗 A tiny numerical duel

Suppose $w = 0.05$. The data-fitting part of the loss is whispering, *"increase w, it helps prediction."* Ridge listens sympathetically — its gradient near zero is nearly nothing, so the two forces reach an easy truce, and the weight just creeps down slowly: $0.010 \to 0.0095 \to 0.0090 \to \dots$ — approaching zero, never quite arriving.

Lasso doesn't negotiate. Its gradient is a flat $+1$ for *any* positive $w$, no matter how small. If the data's pull is weaker than that constant $\lambda$, the penalty simply wins outright, and the weight is driven straight to exactly $0$ — not asymptotically, but in one decisive step of the optimization.

## 🎯 The soft-threshold gate

Lasso is trained with **coordinate descent** — optimizing one coefficient at a time while holding the rest fixed. For each coefficient, the update collapses into a single beautiful operation called **soft-thresholding**:

$$
S(z, \alpha) = \text{sign}(z)\max(|z| - \alpha, 0)
$$

Think of every coefficient walking up to a gate, where $\alpha$ is the threshold:

```
                  LASSO GATE
                      │
          ┌───────────┴───────────┐
          │                       │
      |z| > α                 |z| ≤ α
          │                       │
          ▼                       ▼
   shrink toward 0            become 0
```

If $z=5, \alpha=2$: $S(5,2) = 5-2 = 3$. The coefficient survives, shrunk.
If $z=1, \alpha=2$: $S(1,2) = 0$. The coefficient is gone.

## 🔍 Sanity Check

> "Lasso sees a small coefficient and deletes it" — is that actually what's happening?

Not quite, and this distinction matters. Lasso isn't scanning coefficients one-by-one before training and pre-labeling them "useful" or "useless." All coefficients are optimized *jointly*. A coefficient lands on zero because, at the optimum of the whole objective, $J(0) < J(0.2)$ — the cost of keeping it non-zero exceeds what it buys you in reduced error. It's a consequence of the joint optimization, not a separate filtering step bolted on beforehand.

And it doesn't mean the feature is *useless in the real world*. It means: under this dataset, this scaling, and this $\lambda$, the optimum simply didn't need it.

## 🧭 The geometry, for good measure

For two coefficients, Ridge's constraint $w_1^2+w_2^2 \le t$ draws a **circle**. Lasso's constraint $|w_1|+|w_2|\le t$ draws a **diamond** — with corners sitting directly on the axes, at points like $(0,a)$ or $(a,0)$.

```
      Ridge                    Lasso
       ______                    /\
    .-'      '-.                /  \
  .'            '.             /    \
 /                \            \    /
 \                /             \  /
  '.            .'                \/
    '-.______.-'
```

Error contours are far more likely to strike a *corner* of a diamond than a smooth circle — and striking a corner means one coefficient lands exactly on zero. That's the geometric face of the same story the derivatives already told you.

## 🌗 ML Bridge

This is precisely why Ridge and Lasso get recruited for different jobs. **Ridge** assumes most features carry a little signal and mainly wants to tame instability — it's your tool when multicollinearity is the enemy. **Lasso** assumes only a subset of features actually matter and is willing to zero out the rest — it's your tool when you suspect a sparse truth hiding inside a wide feature set. And when features are *correlated*, Lasso gets moody: given near-duplicate features $x_1 \approx x_2$, it may arbitrarily keep one and zero the other, which is exactly why **Elastic Net** exists — it blends both penalties so correlated groups survive together instead of one twin vanishing at random.

> Explain me about this part.

Strip away the vocabulary and here's what's actually left, in one sentence you can carry into an exam or an interview:

**Ridge** shrinks all coefficients. **Lasso** shrinks *and* deletes some. That's the entire philosophical fork.

## 🌀 Deepest Realization

The corner isn't decoration. It's the mechanism. A smooth curve like $w^2$ has a derivative that *vanishes* as it approaches zero — so the pull weakens exactly when the weight gets small, and the weight just idles near zero forever, never quite arriving. A curve with a corner like $|w|$ has a derivative that *never* weakens as it approaches zero — the force stays at full strength right up to the edge, so when the data's pull is weaker than that constant force, the weight gets shoved straight through zero and locked there.

One tiny geometric feature — a corner instead of a curve — is the entire difference between a model that quietly shrinks everything and a model that's willing to decide a feature doesn't belong.

## ✅ Final Takeaway

> "Ridge continuously shrinks coefficients toward zero, while Lasso uses the L1 penalty whose sharp corner at zero allows coefficients to become exactly zero. L2 shrinks. L1 shrinks *and* eliminates."
