# 🛣️ The Widest Road Between Two Villages: Hyperplanes, Margins & the Hard-Margin SVM

> *"Many lines can separate two classes. Only one of them leaves the most breathing room on both sides. SVM is the algorithm that goes looking for that one."*

You already know that a **Support Vector Machine (SVM)** wants a boundary that stays as far as possible from the nearest training points. In this chapter you will build that idea from the ground up, **proving every step**, until the famous formula

$$\min_{\mathbf w,b}\ \frac12\|\mathbf w\|^2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad y_i(\mathbf w^T\mathbf x_i+b)\ge 1$$

stops looking like something pulled out of a hat and starts looking like the only sensible thing to write down.

We will walk one chain, link by link:

**tools → boundary → why $\mathbf w$ is perpendicular → distance formula → margin planes → why ±1 → one combined constraint → margin width → optimization → support vectors → hand-solved examples → why "hard".**

---

## 🧰 Four Tools You Need in Your Pocket First

Every derivation below uses only these four ideas. Let's make each one concrete so nothing later feels like magic. 🪄

### 1️⃣ The dot product

For two vectors with $d$ entries,

$$\mathbf a^T\mathbf b=a_1b_1+a_2b_2+\dots+a_db_d=\sum_{j=1}^{d}a_jb_j$$

The result is a **single number** (a scalar). Shape check: $\mathbf a^T$ is $1\times d$, $\mathbf b$ is $d\times1$, so the product is $1\times1$.

Example: $\mathbf a=\begin{bmatrix}2\\1\end{bmatrix}$, $\mathbf b=\begin{bmatrix}3\\4\end{bmatrix}$, so $\mathbf a^T\mathbf b=2\cdot3+1\cdot4=10$.

Geometrically, $\mathbf a^T\mathbf b=\|\mathbf a\|\,\|\mathbf b\|\cos\theta$, where $\theta$ is the angle between them. So:

> **Dot product $=0$ ⟺ the vectors are perpendicular** (because $\cos90^\circ=0$).

We will lean on this sentence heavily.

### 2️⃣ The norm (length) of a vector

$$\|\mathbf w\|=\sqrt{w_1^2+w_2^2+\dots+w_d^2}$$

Multiply a vector by itself with the dot product and you get the squared length:

$$\mathbf w^T\mathbf w=w_1^2+\dots+w_d^2=\|\mathbf w\|^2$$

Example: $\mathbf w=\begin{bmatrix}3\\4\end{bmatrix}$ gives $\|\mathbf w\|=\sqrt{9+16}=5$.

### 3️⃣ The unit vector

Dividing a vector by its own length gives a vector of length exactly 1 in the same direction:

$$\mathbf u=\frac{\mathbf w}{\|\mathbf w\|},\qquad \|\mathbf u\|=\frac{\|\mathbf w\|}{\|\mathbf w\|}=1$$

Example: $\mathbf u=\tfrac15\begin{bmatrix}3\\4\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0.6\\0.8\end{bmatrix}$, and $\sqrt{0.6^2+0.8^2}=\sqrt{0.36+0.64}=1$ ✅

Why bother? Because walking $t\,\mathbf u$ moves you **exactly $t$ real units**. Walking $t\,\mathbf w$ would move you $5t$ units, so $t$ would stop being a distance.

### 4️⃣ Projection onto a unit vector

Any vector $\mathbf v$ can be split into a piece along $\mathbf u$ and a piece perpendicular to it:

$$\mathbf v=\alpha\,\mathbf u+\mathbf v_\perp,\qquad \mathbf v_\perp^T\mathbf u=0$$

Take the dot product of both sides with $\mathbf u$:

$$\mathbf v^T\mathbf u=\alpha\underbrace{\mathbf u^T\mathbf u}_{=1}+\underbrace{\mathbf v_\perp^T\mathbf u}_{=0}=\alpha$$

So **the signed length of $\mathbf v$ along a unit direction $\mathbf u$ is simply $\mathbf v^T\mathbf u$.** That is the whole reason we will "project onto the unit normal" later.

## 🧭 The Simplest Possible Picture

Picture two villages on a map.

```
   ●  ●  ●
   ●  ●                 ● = Class +1

--------------------    ← the decision boundary

   ○  ○                 ○ = Class −1
   ○  ○  ○
```

The line between them is the **decision boundary**. Its name depends on the number of features:

| Number of features | What the boundary is called |
|---|---|
| 1 | a point |
| 2 | a line |
| 3 | a plane |
| more than 3 | a **hyperplane** |

Same idea, bigger name tag. 🏷️

## 📐 The Equation of the Boundary

Every hyperplane can be written as

$$\boxed{\mathbf w^T\mathbf x+b=0}$$

Take two features. Then

$$\mathbf x=\begin{bmatrix}x_1\\x_2\end{bmatrix},\qquad \mathbf w=\begin{bmatrix}w_1\\w_2\end{bmatrix}$$

and the dot product expands (tool 1️⃣) to

$$\mathbf w^T\mathbf x=w_1x_1+w_2x_2\;\;\Longrightarrow\;\;w_1x_1+w_2x_2+b=0$$

This is the school-level line $ax+by+c=0$ in new clothes. 👕 With three features it becomes $w_1x_1+w_2x_2+w_3x_3+b=0$, a plane, and so on.

The three symbols:

- **$\mathbf x$**: the input feature vector (one data point).
- **$\mathbf w$**: the **weight vector**.
- **$b$**: the **bias** (a single number).

### 🎯 Reader thought: *"How is the orientation of the plane determined?"*

By $\mathbf w$. Here is the proof that $\mathbf w$ is **perpendicular (normal)** to the hyperplane.

**Step 1.** Pick *any two points* $\mathbf x_a$ and $\mathbf x_b$ that lie on the hyperplane. Both satisfy the equation:

$$\mathbf w^T\mathbf x_a+b=0,\qquad \mathbf w^T\mathbf x_b+b=0$$

**Step 2.** Subtract the second from the first. The $b$'s cancel:

$$\mathbf w^T\mathbf x_a-\mathbf w^T\mathbf x_b=0\;\;\Longrightarrow\;\;\mathbf w^T(\mathbf x_a-\mathbf x_b)=0$$

**Step 3.** The vector $\mathbf x_a-\mathbf x_b$ points *along the surface* of the hyperplane (it joins two points on it). Its dot product with $\mathbf w$ is zero, so by tool 1️⃣ they are perpendicular.

**Step 4.** This works for *every* pair of points on the plane. So $\mathbf w$ is perpendicular to every direction lying inside the plane, which is exactly what "normal to the plane" means. ∎

```
                ↑ w
                │
                │
────────────────┼────────────────
        the hyperplane
```

**Numeric check.** Take the line $3x_1+4x_2-10=0$, so $\mathbf w=(3,4)$, $b=-10$. The points $(2,1)$ and $(0,2.5)$ are both on it: $6+4-10=0$ ✅ and $0+10-10=0$ ✅. Their difference is $(2,-1.5)$, and

$$\mathbf w^T(2,-1.5)=3\cdot2+4\cdot(-1.5)=6-6=0\ ✅$$

So turning $\mathbf w$ turns the whole boundary with it.

### 📍 And what does $b$ do?

Compare

$$2x_1+x_2=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad2x_1+x_2+5=0$$

Both have $\mathbf w=(2,1)$, so both are perpendicular to the same arrow and are therefore **parallel**. Only $b$ differs, and it slides the plane. The next section makes "how far does it slide?" exact.

- $\mathbf w$ controls **orientation** 🧭
- $b$ controls **position** 📍

## 📏 The Master Formula: How Far Is a Point From the Boundary?

Everything in SVM rests on one formula. Let's derive it slowly.

**Goal.** For any point $\mathbf x$, find the signed perpendicular distance $t$ to the hyperplane $\mathbf w^T\mathbf x+b=0$.

**Step 1: Walk from the plane to the point.** Suppose $\mathbf x_0$ lies on the plane and $\mathbf x$ is reached by walking $t$ units along the unit normal $\mathbf u=\mathbf w/\|\mathbf w\|$:

$$\mathbf x=\mathbf x_0+t\,\frac{\mathbf w}{\|\mathbf w\|}$$

**Step 2: Evaluate the score $\mathbf w^T\mathbf x+b$ at that point.**

$$\mathbf w^T\mathbf x+b=\underbrace{\mathbf w^T\mathbf x_0+b}_{=0\ (\mathbf x_0\text{ is on the plane})}+t\,\frac{\mathbf w^T\mathbf w}{\|\mathbf w\|}$$

**Step 3: Use $\mathbf w^T\mathbf w=\|\mathbf w\|^2$ (tool 2️⃣).**

$$\mathbf w^T\mathbf x+b=t\,\frac{\|\mathbf w\|^2}{\|\mathbf w\|}=t\,\|\mathbf w\|$$

**Step 4: Solve for $t$.**

$$\boxed{t=\frac{\mathbf w^T\mathbf x+b}{\|\mathbf w\|}}\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad\boxed{\mathbf w^T\mathbf x+b=t\,\|\mathbf w\|}$$

**Does such an $\mathbf x_0$ always exist?** Yes. Given any $\mathbf x$, set $t=(\mathbf w^T\mathbf x+b)/\|\mathbf w\|$ and define $\mathbf x_0=\mathbf x-t\mathbf u$. Then $\mathbf w^T\mathbf x_0+b=(\mathbf w^T\mathbf x+b)-t\|\mathbf w\|=0$, so $\mathbf x_0$ really is on the plane.

**Is $|t|$ the *shortest* distance?** Let $\mathbf y$ be any other point on the plane. Then $(\mathbf x_0-\mathbf y)$ lies inside the plane, so it is perpendicular to $\mathbf w$ (proved above), and

$$(\mathbf x-\mathbf y)^T\mathbf u=(\mathbf x-\mathbf x_0)^T\mathbf u+(\mathbf x_0-\mathbf y)^T\mathbf u=t+0=t$$

Since a vector's length is at least the size of its component along any unit direction, $\|\mathbf x-\mathbf y\|\ge|t|$. So $|t|$ is the minimum. ∎

**Numeric check.** Plane $3x_1+4x_2-10=0$ and point $(5,5)$:

$$t=\frac{3\cdot5+4\cdot5-10}{5}=\frac{25}{5}=5$$

Independent check with tool 4️⃣: from the plane point $(2,1)$, the vector to $(5,5)$ is $(3,4)$, and $(3,4)\cdot(0.6,0.8)=1.8+3.2=5$ ✅

### 🧩 What this single formula gives us for free

**a) Where $b$ puts the plane.** Distance from the origin ($\mathbf x=\mathbf 0$):

$$t=\frac{b}{\|\mathbf w\|}\;\Longrightarrow\;\text{distance from origin}=\frac{|b|}{\|\mathbf w\|}$$

For $3x_1+4x_2-10=0$ that is $10/5=2$ units. So $b$ slides the plane by an amount tied to $\|\mathbf w\|$.

**b) The prediction rule.** The sign of $t$ tells which side of the plane a point is on, and $|t|$ tells how far:

| Score $f(\mathbf x)=\mathbf w^T\mathbf x+b$ | Side of boundary | Prediction |
|---|---|---|
| $f>0$ | the side $\mathbf w$ points toward | $+1$ |
| $f<0$ | the opposite side | $-1$ |
| $f=0$ | on the boundary | undecided |

$$\boxed{\hat y=\operatorname{sign}(\mathbf w^T\mathbf x+b)}$$

And for a training point with label $y_i$, the **geometric distance** to the boundary (positive if it is on its correct side) is

$$\boxed{\text{dist}_i=\frac{y_i(\mathbf w^T\mathbf x_i+b)}{\|\mathbf w\|}}$$

Keep this one in mind. It reappears in a moment. 🔑

## 🤔 Which Line Do We Pick?

Look at data that is cleanly separable:

```
  ● ● ●

     ───────────   ← Boundary A
        ─────────  ← Boundary B
           ───────  ← Boundary C

                  ○ ○ ○
```

All three classify every training point correctly. So which one should SVM choose?

Its answer:

> **"Choose the boundary with the widest empty street around it."** 🛣️

The empty region on either side is the **margin**. The training points touching the edge of that street are the **support vectors**. They *support* (fix the position of) the boundary.

```
Class +1   ●      ●
                 │
    ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─│─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─   ← margin edge (touches support vectors)
    ═════════════════════════    ← decision boundary
    ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─│─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─   ← margin edge (touches support vectors)
                 │
Class −1   ○      ○
```

### 🏠 A mental model

Two groups of houses, and you are building a road between them. You want the road to separate the groups, be centred between them, and be as wide as possible. The houses closest to the road decide where it can go. Those are the support vectors. Houses far away could be deleted and the road would not move. 🚧

---

## ➕➖ Drawing the Two Edges of the Street

To turn "a wide street" into algebra, SVM defines **two more hyperplanes**, parallel to the boundary:

$$\boxed{\mathbf w^T\mathbf x+b=+1}\quad\text{(passes through the closest positive points)}$$

$$\boxed{\mathbf w^T\mathbf x+b=-1}\quad\text{(passes through the closest negative points)}$$

```
        wᵀx + b =  1   ════════════   (positive support vectors sit here)
        wᵀx + b =  0   ────────────   decision boundary
        wᵀx + b = -1   ════════════   (negative support vectors sit here)
```

**They are parallel** because all three equations share the same $\mathbf w$ (a plane's orientation is fixed by $\mathbf w$, as proved above).

**They are equally far from the boundary.** By the master formula, a point with score $+1$ has $t=\dfrac{1}{\|\mathbf w\|}$, and a point with score $-1$ has $t=-\dfrac{1}{\|\mathbf w\|}$. Same distance, opposite sides. So the decision boundary sits exactly in the **middle** of the street. 🎯

### 🎯 Reader thought: *"Why exactly +1 and −1? Why not +5, or +100?"*

Because the numbers $\mathbf w$ and $b$ have a hidden **scaling freedom**, so the constant on the right is ours to choose. Let's prove it in two steps.

**Step 1: Scaling $(\mathbf w,b)$ does not move the boundary.** For any $c>0$:

$$(c\mathbf w)^T\mathbf x+cb=c(\mathbf w^T\mathbf x+b)=0\;\iff\;\mathbf w^T\mathbf x+b=0$$

Same set of points, so same plane. The predictions $\operatorname{sign}(\cdot)$ also do not change, because multiplying by $c>0$ never flips a sign.

Numeric check: $(3,4),\,b=-10$ and $(15,20),\,b=-50$ describe the same line. (In the sklearn run later, multiplying $\mathbf w$ and $b$ by 5 left every predicted sign unchanged.)

**Step 2: Any constant can be rescaled to 1.** Suppose the margin planes were $\mathbf w^T\mathbf x+b=\pm5$. Divide through by 5:

$$\left(\frac{\mathbf w}{5}\right)^T\mathbf x+\frac b5=\pm1$$

With $\mathbf w'=\mathbf w/5$ and $b'=b/5$ we get the same planes at $\pm1$.

**Why we are *allowed* to do this for a real dataset.** Take any separating $(\mathbf w,b)$. Every point has $y_i(\mathbf w^T\mathbf x_i+b)>0$. Let the smallest of these values be $m>0$ (attained by the closest point). Replace $(\mathbf w,b)$ by $(\mathbf w/m,\,b/m)$. The boundary is unchanged (Step 1), and now

$$\min_i\;y_i(\mathbf w^T\mathbf x_i+b)=1$$

> The ±1 is therefore a **normalization convention**: it pins down the arbitrary scale so that the closest points sit at score exactly 1. It is a choice of ruler, not a law of nature.

## 🔗 Two Rules Become One

Each training point is $(\mathbf x_i,y_i)$ with $y_i\in\{-1,+1\}$. We want every point **correctly classified and outside the street**, not merely on the correct side of the boundary.

- Positive points: $\ \mathbf w^T\mathbf x_i+b\ \ge\ 1$
- Negative points: $\ \mathbf w^T\mathbf x_i+b\ \le\ -1$

Two inequalities are clumsy. Multiply each by its own label $y_i$ and they merge:

$$\boxed{y_i(\mathbf w^T\mathbf x_i+b)\ \ge\ 1\qquad \forall\, i=1,\dots,n}$$

**Proof that nothing was lost.**

1. If $y_i=+1$: $(+1)(\mathbf w^T\mathbf x_i+b)\ge1\Rightarrow\mathbf w^T\mathbf x_i+b\ge1$ ✅
2. If $y_i=-1$: $(-1)(\mathbf w^T\mathbf x_i+b)\ge1$. Dividing both sides by $-1$ **reverses** the inequality (rule: multiplying or dividing by a negative flips $\ge$ to $\le$), giving $\mathbf w^T\mathbf x_i+b\le-1$ ✅

**What the constraint means geometrically.** Using the geometric distance from earlier, $\text{dist}_i=y_i(\mathbf w^T\mathbf x_i+b)/\|\mathbf w\|$, the constraint says

$$\text{dist}_i\ \ge\ \frac{1}{\|\mathbf w\|}$$

that is, **every point must be at least half a street-width away from the boundary**. Three cases:

| Value of $y_i(\mathbf w^T\mathbf x_i+b)$ | Where the point is |
|---|---|
| exactly $1$ | on a margin edge, so it is a **support vector** |
| greater than $1$ | correctly classified, comfortably outside the street |
| less than $1$ | inside the street, or misclassified. **Forbidden** here |

## 📏 How Wide Is the Street?

Now the headline derivation. We have three independent ways to reach the same answer, and seeing all three is the best way to trust it.

### 🎯 Reader thought: *"Why does the unit vector show up? Doesn't its size decide the margin?"*

This is a very common mix-up, so slow down here.

**A unit vector's size is always 1** ($\|\mathbf u\|=1$). It never decides anything by its *size*. Its only job is to give the **perpendicular direction** while keeping distances honest (tool 3️⃣). What sets the width is $\|\mathbf w\|$, and the derivations below show exactly how.

### Derivation A: Project the gap between two support vectors

Pick one support vector on each margin plane.

- $\mathbf x_1$ on the negative plane:
$$\mathbf w^T\mathbf x_1+b=-1\;\Longrightarrow\;\mathbf w^T\mathbf x_1=-1-b$$
- $\mathbf x_2$ on the positive plane:
$$\mathbf w^T\mathbf x_2+b=1\;\Longrightarrow\;\mathbf w^T\mathbf x_2=1-b$$

The vector joining them is $\mathbf x_2-\mathbf x_1$. It may be slanted, but the street's width is measured *straight across*, along the normal direction $\mathbf u=\mathbf w/\|\mathbf w\|$. By tool 4️⃣, that component is the dot product with $\mathbf u$:

$$d=(\mathbf x_2-\mathbf x_1)^T\frac{\mathbf w}{\|\mathbf w\|}=\frac{\mathbf w^T\mathbf x_2-\mathbf w^T\mathbf x_1}{\|\mathbf w\|}$$

(The step uses that $(\mathbf x_2-\mathbf x_1)^T\mathbf w=\mathbf w^T\mathbf x_2-\mathbf w^T\mathbf x_1$, by distributing the dot product.)

Substitute the two results above:

$$d=\frac{(1-b)-(-1-b)}{\|\mathbf w\|}=\frac{1-b+1+b}{\|\mathbf w\|}$$

The $b$'s cancel, which is a nice sign that the width does not depend on where the street sits, only on its orientation and scale:

$$\boxed{d=\frac{2}{\|\mathbf w\|}}$$

### Derivation B: Walk out from the boundary

From the master formula, the score after walking $t$ units from the boundary is $t\,\|\mathbf w\|$.

- To reach the positive edge, the score must be $1$: $\ t\|\mathbf w\|=1\Rightarrow t=\dfrac{1}{\|\mathbf w\|}$.
- To reach the negative edge, the score must be $-1$: $\ t=-\dfrac{1}{\|\mathbf w\|}$, which is the same distance in the opposite direction.

Total width:

$$\frac{1}{\|\mathbf w\|}+\frac{1}{\|\mathbf w\|}=\frac{2}{\|\mathbf w\|}\quad✅$$

### Derivation C: One line from the distance formula

Half-width $=$ distance of a support vector from the boundary $=\dfrac{|{\pm1}|}{\|\mathbf w\|}=\dfrac1{\|\mathbf w\|}$. Double it. Same answer. ✅

### 🎢 So what is $\|\mathbf w\|$, really?

From $\mathbf w^T\mathbf x+b=t\,\|\mathbf w\|$:

> **$\|\mathbf w\|$ is the rate at which the score changes per unit of perpendicular distance.**

- If $\|\mathbf w\|=10$: each unit step changes the score by 10, so going from score $0$ to $1$ takes only $0.1$ units. **Thin street.**
- If $\|\mathbf w\|=2$: going from $0$ to $1$ takes $0.5$ units. **Wide street.**

$$\boxed{\text{large }\|\mathbf w\|\Rightarrow\text{narrow margin}\qquad\qquad\text{small }\|\mathbf w\|\Rightarrow\text{wide margin}}$$

So: **the unit vector supplies the direction, and $\|\mathbf w\|$ supplies the steepness.** Together they give the width.

*Ramp analogy:* a steep ramp raises the reading quickly, so you only need a short walk to gain one unit. A gentle ramp needs a long walk. The margin is that "long walk". 🎿

## 🔄 From Geometry to an Optimization Problem

We want the widest street:

$$\max_{\mathbf w,b}\ \frac{2}{\|\mathbf w\|}$$

### Step 1: Maximizing $2/\|\mathbf w\|$ is the same as minimizing $\|\mathbf w\|$

The function $g(s)=2/s$ is **decreasing** for $s>0$: a bigger $s$ gives a smaller $g$. So the $\mathbf w$ that makes $2/\|\mathbf w\|$ largest is the $\mathbf w$ that makes $\|\mathbf w\|$ smallest.

Is $\|\mathbf w\|>0$ guaranteed (so we never divide by zero)? Yes. If $\mathbf w=\mathbf 0$, the constraint becomes $y_i(0+b)=y_ib\ge1$ for all $i$. That needs $b\ge1$ (for $y_i=+1$) *and* $b\le-1$ (for $y_i=-1$), which is impossible when both classes exist. So $\mathbf w\neq\mathbf 0$.

### Step 2: Minimizing $\|\mathbf w\|$ is the same as minimizing $\|\mathbf w\|^2$

The squaring function $s\mapsto s^2$ is increasing for $s\ge0$, so it preserves order: if $\|\mathbf w_A\|<\|\mathbf w_B\|$ then $\|\mathbf w_A\|^2<\|\mathbf w_B\|^2$. The **minimizer is the same**. We square because $\|\mathbf w\|^2=w_1^2+\dots+w_d^2$ has no square root, which makes differentiation clean.

### Step 3: Why the $\tfrac12$?

Take the gradient (the vector of partial derivatives) of $\tfrac12\|\mathbf w\|^2=\tfrac12\sum_j w_j^2$:

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial w_j}\left(\frac12\sum_k w_k^2\right)=\frac12\cdot2w_j=w_j\;\;\Longrightarrow\;\;\nabla_{\mathbf w}\left(\frac12\|\mathbf w\|^2\right)=\mathbf w$$

Without the $\tfrac12$ you would carry a stray factor of 2 through every later step. Multiplying an objective by a positive constant never changes where its minimum is.

### Step 4: Why this is a "nice" problem (convexity)

- The second derivative (Hessian) of $\tfrac12\|\mathbf w\|^2$ with respect to $\mathbf w$ is the identity matrix $I$, which is positive definite. So the objective is a **strictly convex bowl**.
- Each constraint $y_i(\mathbf w^T\mathbf x_i+b)\ge1$ is **linear** in $(\mathbf w,b)$, so each one describes a half-space, and their intersection is a convex region.
- A convex bowl over a convex region has no misleading local minima: a minimum you find is the global one. This is a **convex quadratic program**.

### Putting the pieces together

$$\boxed{\begin{aligned}\min_{\mathbf w,b}\quad&\frac12\|\mathbf w\|^2\\[4pt]\text{subject to}\quad&y_i(\mathbf w^T\mathbf x_i+b)\ge1,\quad i=1,\dots,n\end{aligned}}$$

Read it as a sentence:

> *"Make the weight vector as small as possible (widest road), **while** keeping every single house outside the road."*

This is a **Constrained Optimization Problem**, and it is the **Hard-Margin SVM**.

## 🧲 Why the Support Vectors Must Touch the Edge

You may have noticed that support vectors have score *exactly* $1$. Is that a coincidence? No. Here is the proof by contradiction.

**Claim.** At the optimum, at least one constraint holds with equality: $y_i(\mathbf w^T\mathbf x_i+b)=1$ for some $i$.

**Proof.**

1. Suppose $(\mathbf w^\*,b^\*)$ is optimal but **every** constraint is strict: $y_i(\mathbf w^{\*T}\mathbf x_i+b^\*)>1$.
2. Let $m=\min_i y_i(\mathbf w^{\*T}\mathbf x_i+b^\*)$. By assumption, $m>1$.
3. Build a new candidate $(\mathbf w',b')=(\mathbf w^\*/m,\;b^\*/m)$.
4. Every constraint value is divided by $m$, so the smallest becomes exactly $1$ and the rest are $\ge1$. The new candidate is **feasible**. ✅
5. Its objective is $\tfrac12\|\mathbf w^\*\|^2/m^2$, which is **strictly smaller** than $\tfrac12\|\mathbf w^\*\|^2$ because $m>1$.
6. That contradicts optimality of $(\mathbf w^\*,b^\*)$. So the assumption was false. ∎

**Interpretation.** If nothing touches the street's edge, the street can still be widened, so it was not the widest. The points that end up touching the edges are the support vectors. (Why there is one touching point on **each** side, and why only those points matter, comes out cleanly from the KKT conditions in the next chapter.)

## ✍️ Solve It by Hand: A One-Dimensional Example

Let's actually solve a hard-margin SVM with pencil and paper.

**Data (1 feature):** $x=1$ with label $y=-1$, and $x=3$ with label $y=+1$. Here $w$ and $b$ are single numbers.

**Constraints** $y_i(wx_i+b)\ge1$:

- Point 1: $\ (-1)(w\cdot1+b)\ge1\;\Rightarrow\;w+b\le-1$
- Point 2: $\ (+1)(w\cdot3+b)\ge1\;\Rightarrow\;3w+b\ge1$

**Step 1.** Subtract: $(3w+b)-(w+b)\ge1-(-1)$, so $2w\ge2$, that is $w\ge1$.

**Step 2.** We minimize $\tfrac12w^2$ with $w\ge1$. The smallest allowed positive $w$ is $w=1$.

**Step 3.** With $w=1$: $\ 1+b\le-1\Rightarrow b\le-2$, and $3+b\ge1\Rightarrow b\ge-2$. So $b=-2$.

**Result.** The boundary is $x-2=0$, i.e. $x=2$, exactly halfway between the two points. Both constraints are tight (score $-1$ at $x=1$, score $+1$ at $x=3$), and

$$\text{margin}=\frac{2}{|w|}=2,$$

which equals the actual gap between the points $3-1=2$. ✅ The solution is what intuition predicts, now proved.

## ✍️ Solve It by Hand: A Two-Dimensional Example

**Data.**

| Class | Points |
|---|---|
| $-1$ | $(1,1),\ (2,1),\ (1,2)$ |
| $+1$ | $(4,4),\ (5,4),\ (4,5)$ |

By eye, the closest pairs are around $(2,1)$, $(1,2)$ and $(4,4)$. **Assume** these three are the support vectors, so their constraints are tight (equalities). This assumption is later *confirmed* by checking the remaining constraints, and proven in general by KKT.

**Three equations** (with $\mathbf w=(w_1,w_2)$):

$$\begin{aligned}
2w_1+w_2+b&=-1\quad&(2,1)\\
w_1+2w_2+b&=-1\quad&(1,2)\\
4w_1+4w_2+b&=+1\quad&(4,4)
\end{aligned}$$

**Step 1.** Subtract the second from the first: $w_1-w_2=0$, so $w_1=w_2=a$.

**Step 2.** Substitute into the first and third:

$$3a+b=-1,\qquad 8a+b=1$$

**Step 3.** Subtract: $5a=2\Rightarrow a=0.4$. Then $b=-1-3(0.4)=-2.2$.

$$\mathbf w=\begin{bmatrix}0.4\\0.4\end{bmatrix},\qquad b=-2.2$$

**Step 4: Check the constraints that were *not* assumed tight.**

| Point | $y$ | $\mathbf w^T\mathbf x+b$ | $y(\mathbf w^T\mathbf x+b)$ | OK? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $(1,1)$ | $-1$ | $0.8-2.2=-1.4$ | $1.4$ | ✅ $\ge1$ |
| $(2,1)$ | $-1$ | $1.2-2.2=-1.0$ | $1.0$ | ✅ tight |
| $(1,2)$ | $-1$ | $1.2-2.2=-1.0$ | $1.0$ | ✅ tight |
| $(4,4)$ | $+1$ | $3.2-2.2=+1.0$ | $1.0$ | ✅ tight |
| $(5,4)$ | $+1$ | $3.6-2.2=+1.4$ | $1.4$ | ✅ $\ge1$ |
| $(4,5)$ | $+1$ | $3.6-2.2=+1.4$ | $1.4$ | ✅ $\ge1$ |

**Step 5: Margin.**

$$\|\mathbf w\|=\sqrt{0.4^2+0.4^2}=\sqrt{0.32}\approx0.5657,\qquad \text{margin}=\frac{2}{0.5657}\approx3.536$$

**Independent geometric check** (Derivation A on real points): the gap between support vectors $(2,1)$ and $(4,4)$ is $(2,3)$. Project it onto the unit normal $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,1)$:

$$\frac{2+3}{\sqrt2}=\frac{5}{\sqrt2}\approx3.536\quad✅$$

Two different routes, one number. 🎉

## 🧱 Why Is It Called "Hard"?

Because the constraints are **non-negotiable**.

- It assumes the data is **perfectly linearly separable**.
- It does not allow **one** point to be misclassified.
- It does not allow **one** point to sit inside the street.

Tidy data works:

```
● ● ●
● ● ●
────────────      ← a clean line exists
○ ○ ○
○ ○ ○
```

Real data has noise, outliers, mislabelled rows and overlapping classes:

```
● ● ●
● ● ●
        ○
         ●   ← one troublesome point
       ○ ○
       ○ ○
```

### 🔍 A proof that the problem can have *no solution*

Take the smallest possible unseparable case, in one dimension:

| $x$ | $y$ |
|---|---|
| $0$ | $+1$ |
| $1$ | $-1$ |
| $2$ | $+1$ |

(A single threshold on the $x$-axis cannot put the middle point alone on one side.) The constraints $y_i(wx_i+b)\ge1$ read:

$$\begin{aligned}
(+1)(w\cdot0+b)\ge1&\;\Rightarrow\;b\ge1&&(1)\\
(-1)(w\cdot1+b)\ge1&\;\Rightarrow\;w+b\le-1&&(2)\\
(+1)(w\cdot2+b)\ge1&\;\Rightarrow\;2w+b\ge1&&(3)
\end{aligned}$$

From (2): $w\le-1-b$. Combine with (1), $b\ge1$, to get $w\le-2$. Now push that into the left side of (3):

$$2w+b\le2(-1-b)+b=-2-b\le-3$$

Constraint (3) demands $2w+b\ge1$, but we just showed $2w+b\le-3$. **Contradiction.** No pair $(w,b)$ exists. The feasible region is empty, so the optimization has nothing to minimize over. 🚫

Hard-margin SVM does not degrade gracefully: it simply returns no answer. The cure is the **Soft-Margin SVM**, which adds *slack variables* so that some points may violate the margin at a price.

## ✅ Sanity Check: Do the Formulas Hold on Real Numbers?

Never trust a derivation you have not poked with data. The same 2D dataset, fitted with scikit-learn's `SVC(kernel="linear", C=1e10)` (a huge `C` approximates a hard margin):

| Quantity | Hand solution | scikit-learn fit |
|---|---|---|
| $\mathbf w$ | $(0.4,\ 0.4)$ | $\approx(0.400,\ 0.400)$ ✅ |
| $b$ | $-2.2$ | $\approx-2.200$ ✅ |
| $\|\mathbf w\|$ | $0.5657$ | $\approx0.5657$ ✅ |
| Margin $2/\|\mathbf w\|$ | $3.536$ | $\approx3.536$ ✅ |
| Support vectors | $(2,1),(1,2),(4,4)$ | $(2,1),(1,2),(4,4)$ ✅ |
| Distance of each support vector to boundary | $\tfrac12\cdot3.536=1.768$ | $\approx1.768$ ✅ |

Scaling-freedom check: multiplying the fitted $\mathbf w$ and $b$ by 5 leaves every predicted sign unchanged, as Step 1 of the ±1 argument predicts. ✅

## 🌉 ML Bridge

Imagine the **Stroke Prediction** setting with just two features, say age and average glucose level. Each patient is a point $\mathbf x=(\text{age},\text{glucose})$ and the label is $y=+1$ (stroke) or $y=-1$ (no stroke).

- The hyperplane $\mathbf w^T\mathbf x+b=0$ is a line in the age-glucose plane. Adding more clinical features raises the dimension, and it becomes a hyperplane.
- The score $\mathbf w^T\mathbf x+b$ divided by $\|\mathbf w\|$ is the patient's signed distance from the boundary, a natural "how far from the border" measure.
- The support vectors are the borderline patients closest to the boundary. Patients far away can be removed without moving it.
- Features on very different scales (age in tens, glucose in hundreds) change $\|\mathbf w\|$ and therefore the geometry, which is why **feature scaling** matters for SVM.

There is also a warning hidden here. Real medical data almost never satisfies $y_i(\mathbf w^T\mathbf x_i+b)\ge1$ for every patient, because noise and overlapping cases are the rule. Hard-margin SVM is the **concept**, and soft-margin is what you will use in practice.

## 🌀 Deepest Realization

The SVM formula is a **geometric wish translated into algebra**:

| The wish | The algebra |
|---|---|
| "Every point must stay outside the street" | $y_i(\mathbf w^T\mathbf x_i+b)\ge1$ |
| "The street must be as wide as possible" | $\max\ \dfrac{2}{\|\mathbf w\|}$ |
| "...which is the same as a small weight vector" | $\min\ \tfrac12\|\mathbf w\|^2$ |

The mysterious ±1 is only a **ruler**: it fixes the scale of $\mathbf w$ so that the width reads off directly as $2/\|\mathbf w\|$. The quantity $\|\mathbf w\|$ is not a size to be admired, it is a **rate of change**: how fast the score climbs as you walk away from the boundary. A steep climb means a thin street, and a gentle climb means a wide one. 🛣️

---

## ✅ Final Takeaway

> **"A hyperplane is $\mathbf w^T\mathbf x+b=0$: $\mathbf w$ is perpendicular to it (subtract two points on the plane and the dot product is zero), and a point's signed distance from it is $\frac{\mathbf w^T\mathbf x+b}{\|\mathbf w\|}$. Normalizing the closest scores to $\pm1$ makes the street exactly $\frac{2}{\|\mathbf w\|}$ wide, so the widest road means the smallest $\|\mathbf w\|$. The Hard-Margin SVM is therefore: minimize $\frac12\|\mathbf w\|^2$ while every point satisfies $y_i(\mathbf w^T\mathbf x_i+b)\ge1$, which is possible only when the data can be perfectly separated."**
